*By Dr. Melina Perdikopoulou Melpomeni | Centre for Byzantine Research, Aristotle University of Thessaloniki* 

**Overview** 

The Ottoman transformation of Thessaloniki between the 15th and 17th centuries can be vividly traced through three emblematic monuments: the Bedesten, the Great Caravanserai, and the Hamza Bey Camii. Together, they formed a coherent urban ensemble that redefined the city’s commercial, religious, and social landscape. Located at the crossing of the decumanus maximus (Egnatia) and the north–south axis leading to the port (modern Venizelou Street), these monuments anchored the Ottomanization of Thessaloniki’s historic core and reveal the fusion of commerce, mobility, and piety at the heart of the city.

*The Bedesten: Commerce and Security*

The Bedesten of Thessaloniki, erected most likely during the reign of Bayezid II (r. 1481–1512) but possibly as early as Mehmed II’s rule (r. 1444–46/ 1451–81), was one of the earliest Ottoman monuments in the city. This domed, stone-built covered market functioned both as a state-controlled institution and a waqf endowment, ensuring revenue for charitable foundations. It was the repository of the city’s most precious goods—jewelry, coins, textiles, and arms—and the site where public transactions, exchange rates, and quality controls were carried out. Taxes on commercial activities were also collected here. The traveler Evliya Çelebi, visiting in the 1660s, described the Bedesten as filled with “the fragrance of precious goods and wealthy merchants counting their gold.” As in other Ottoman centers, the Bedesten symbolized both prosperity and security, embodying the sultan’s authority over economic life.

*The Great Caravanserai: Mobility and Hospitality*

The Great Caravanserai, founded by Mustafa Paşa, beylerbey of Rumelia (1498–1505), was recorded in his waqf alongside shops, mills, and villages by 1546. Situated directly above the Hamza Bey Camii at the Egnatia–Venizelou crossing, the building later gave way to the 20th-century Town Hall. Architecturally, it followed the typical Ottoman caravanserai layout: an elongated rectangular courtyard surrounded by two-storied galleries with rooms and shops, equipped with fireplaces, storage areas, and stables. Caravanserais offered shelter for merchants and travelers who were entitled to food and lodging for up to three days, reinforcing the city’s role as a nodal point in Mediterranean and Balkan trade networks. Its location confirmed the continuity of Thessaloniki’s Byzantine commercial center while also integrating it into the Ottoman imperial road and caravan systems.

*Hamza Bey Camii: From Mescid to Monumental Mosque*

The Hamza Bey Camii, also known as Alkazar, was erected around 1467/68 as a modest mescid (small prayer hall) dedicated by the Evrenos family. In the early 16th century, it was expanded into a full-fledged mosque with a surrounding enclosure—the only such example in the Greek lands—underscoring its elevated status in the city’s religious and social hierarchy. Positioned at the same central crossroads as the Bedesten and Caravanserai, Hamza Bey Camii created a sacred counterpoint to commercial and mercantile institutions, fusing piety and economy within a single urban hub. Evliya Çelebi emphasized its constant use, remarking in 1668 that “day and night, worshippers never cease to gather here.”

Archaeological excavations that were carried out during the construction of the Thessaloniki metro (2015-19) have illuminated the deeper historical significance of this crossroads in the city. At the Venizelou and Agia Sophia stations, archaeologists uncovered workshops, storage facilities, and shops dating to the late Byzantine era and through the 19th century. These included ceramic kilns, metallurgical furnaces, jewelry-making facilities, and glass workshops, many with street-front shops facing Egnatia. Such findings reveal the area as a continuous artisan and commercial zone for centuries, where production and exchange were intimately linked to the city’s main artery. At Venizelou, a monumental late Byzantine building, in use until the 18th century, was uncovered. It may have been integrated into the early Ottoman urban fabric, further underscoring the significance of this locus. The Hamza Bey Camii thus did not arise in isolation but was embedded within a longstanding commercial landscape, reinforcing the continuity of Thessaloniki’s economic and urban traditions under Ottoman rule.

Taken together, the Bedesten, the Great Caravanserai, and the Hamza Bey Camii formed an interdependent triad at the heart of Ottoman Thessaloniki. Their proximity created a dynamic zone where trade, lodging, and worship intersected, a microcosm of the Ottoman city’s ideals of balance between material prosperity and spiritual life. The choice of location—at the intersection of the city’s principal Roman axes, near the port, and within walking distance of other key institutions such as the Bey Hamam—demonstrates how the Ottomans appropriated and reconfigured the Byzantine urban grid to project imperial authority and accommodate new economic and religious realities.

The study of these monuments rests on a wide range of sources: Ottoman waqf deeds, tax registers, and court records; travelers’ accounts such as those of Evliya Çelebi; archaeological data from the metro excavations; and the surviving fabric of the monuments themselves. Together, these materials and sources enable scholars to reconstruct the complex layering of Thessaloniki’s urban life across the transition from Byzantine to Ottoman rule.

The thematic significance of this cluster of monuments lies not only in their architectural and functional roles but also in what they reveal about broader processes of Ottomanization, the resilience of commercial infrastructures, and the enduring centrality of Thessaloniki in regional networks. Today, these monuments, fragmented and often reinterpreted through modern heritage discourses, continue to embody the city’s layered history of commerce, mobility, and faith.

**Key Issues and Debates** 

The study of Thessaloniki’s Ottoman monuments—the Bedesten, the Great Caravanserai, and the Hamza Bey Camii—has generated sustained scholarly interest and debate. These buildings, concentrated around the central crossroads of the Egnatia and the axis leading to the harbor, constitute an integrated ensemble that reveals the strategies of Ottoman urbanism, the mechanisms of commerce and piety, as well as the transformation of a Byzantine metropolis into an Ottoman imperial city. Their analysis engages a wide spectrum of sources, from Ottoman archival documents and endowment deeds (*vakfiyes*) to the testimony of travelers, court registers, archaeological excavations, and architectural surveys. Each category of evidence has been read differently across historiographical traditions, resulting in divergent interpretations of chronology, function, and meaning.

A central issue has been the chronology and patronage of these monuments. The Bedesten is generally attributed to the reign of Bayezid II (r. 1481–1512), although some scholars, relying on tax registers and early references, argue for an earlier foundation under Mehmed II in the 1450s. This debate reflects broader uncertainties about the timing of the consolidation of Ottoman control in Thessaloniki and the pace at which monumental investment followed conquest. The Great Caravanserai is firmly linked to the patronage of Mustafa Paşa, beylerbey of Rumelia, in the early 16th century, but discussion continues as to the extent of his endowment and the integration of this structure into a larger waqf complex. The Hamza Bey Camii presents further complexity: originally founded as a mescid in 1467/68 by members of the Evrenos family, it was later expanded into a congregational mosque with a walled precinct, a rare typology in the Greek lands, as precincts were built only for sultanic mosques and especially in the three capitals of the empire (Bursa, Edirne and Istanbul). The evolution from mescid to mosque underscores the adaptive growth of the Muslim community and the layering of patronage within Thessaloniki’s urban core.

The relationship between Ottoman monuments and Byzantine urban fabric constitutes another major field of debate. The placement of the Bedesten, caravanserai, and Hamza Bey Camii at the intersection of the city’s decumanus maximus (Egnatia) and its cardo (modern Venizelou Street) has been interpreted as a deliberate appropriation of the Byzantine and Roman street grid. Scholars disagree on whether this should be seen as an assertion of continuity, with Ottomans preserving an established commercial hub, or as a conscious imperial strategy of redefinition, overlaying Islamic institutions upon the symbolic heart of the Byzantine city. Archaeological excavations undertaken during the construction of the Thessaloniki metro have added new evidence to this discussion, revealing workshops, kilns, and commercial facilities active from the late Byzantine period into the nineteenth century. These findings support the notion of long-term continuity of craft and trade in the area, while also illustrating how Ottoman monuments became nodes within an evolving economic landscape.

Closely tied to urban integration is the question of economic and social function. The bedesten is understood as both marketplace and treasury, a space where precious goods were stored, inspected, and transacted under the supervision of state-appointed officials. Its role in regulating coinage, assaying metals, and hosting notarial acts has been emphasized in recent studies, which see the building as a guarantor of commercial trust and as a visible symbol of state authority within the economy. The Great Caravanserai, by contrast, has been studied as a locus of mobility, providing lodging and services for merchants and travelers, and thereby linking Thessaloniki to wider Ottoman and Mediterranean trade routes. Its integration into Mustafa Paşa’s vakıf highlights the economic logic of endowment, where revenue-generating properties underpinned religious and charitable functions. The Hamza Bey Camii, with its substantial precinct, embodied the pious dimension of urban life, anchoring a quarter while simultaneously serving as the focal point for markets and caravan traffic. Together, these institutions demonstrate the interweaving of commerce and piety within the Ottoman city.

The role of vakıf institutions has been the subject of broader historiographical debate. Scholars have variously stressed their conservative, immobilizing function—tying resources to perpetual charitable use—or their dynamic role in financing urban development and enabling the circulation of wealth. The Thessaloniki ensemble provides fertile ground for examining these questions, since the revenues of both the Bedesten and caravanserai were directed toward sustaining mosques, madrasas, and other pious foundations. The linkage between commercial and religious architecture in a single endowment illuminates the dual character of Ottoman vakıf practice as both economic engine and moral framework.

The multiethnic and multireligious composition of Thessaloniki has further shaped interpretations. By the late 15th century, the influx of Sephardic Jews after 1492 created one of the largest Jewish communities in the Ottoman world, alongside significant Christian and Muslim populations. Scholars have debated how far the Ottoman-built environment facilitated coexistence or reinforced communal boundaries. The location of the Bedesten and caravanserai near the city’s Jewish quarters has been read as evidence of integration into shared commercial networks, while the monumental mosque expressed the primacy of Islam within the imperial order. These questions intersect with larger debates on the character of Ottoman urban society: whether it was primarily segregated along confessional lines or more fluidly interconnected through trade, craft, and neighborhood relations.

The archaeological record continues to complicate and enrich the picture. Excavations linked to the metro have brought to light artisanal installations, storage areas, and traces of earlier Byzantine structures beneath or adjacent to the Ottoman monuments. These findings suggest that the Ottoman investments did not erase the past but rather overlaid and adapted pre-existing commercial structures. The persistence of craft specializations—such as jewelry-making, ceramics, and metallurgy—into the Ottoman period highlights the resilience of Thessaloniki’s artisanal traditions, while also underscoring the new institutional frameworks, such as guilds and vakıf management, through which they operated. The combination of written and material evidence has therefore prompted scholars to reconsider rigid narratives of rupture and replacement.

Modern scholarship has also engaged with the heritage and memory of these monuments. The Bedesten, long integrated into the city’s commercial life, survives today as a market hall, while the site of the caravanserai has been largely obliterated, with only archival references preserving its memory. The Hamza Bey Camii, known in modern times as the Alkazar after the adjacent cinema, has undergone restoration and re-evaluation as an architectural monument that is expected to open for the public in 2026. Debates surrounding these sites reflect larger tensions in the reception of Ottoman heritage in Greece, oscillating between neglect, utilitarian adaptation, and preservation as cultural assets. The contested meanings of these monuments in the present underscore the enduring resonance of Ottoman urban legacies in Thessaloniki’s identity.

Finally, the Thessaloniki ensemble contributes to wider debates in Ottoman and Mediterranean urban history. Comparative perspectives have situated the city’s Bedesten alongside those of Istanbul, Edirne, and Bursa, highlighting similarities in form and function but also distinctive features tied to local context. The Hamza Bey Camii’s transformation from mescid to mosque with precinct exemplifies the processes of institutional maturation in provincial centers. The Great Caravanserai, as part of the larger network of khans and inns, demonstrates the infrastructural underpinnings of long-distance commerce. By studying these monuments together, scholars illuminate how imperial strategies were localized, how commerce and religion were entwined, and how a Byzantine city was reshaped into an Ottoman metropolis. This coexistence of commerce and religion, manifested in the juxtaposition of the bedesten, the mosque, and the caravanserai, strategically arranged around a central crossroads, reflects a spatial logic that had already been established in the Roman and Byzantine periods, when the same area was imbued with commercial and civic roles. The Ottoman interventions did not simply overwrite the earlier topography but rather reactivated and redefined it within a new religious and socio-economic framework. What is particularly striking is that this specific constellation of buildings—combining mercantile, religious, and hospitable functions within a single nodal point of the urban fabric—has not been attested in any other provincial city of the Ottoman Empire, thus underscoring the distinctive character of this urban arrangement.

The implications of this scholarship extend beyond architectural history. They invite reflection on the dynamics of continuity and change in urban life, the relationship between economy and piety, and the negotiation of identity within a pluralistic society. The debates around chronology, function, and memory show how different kinds of evidence—texts, architecture, and archaeology—can be brought into dialogue. At the same time, they reveal the stakes of interpreting Ottoman heritage in contemporary Greece, where these monuments have become touchstones of cultural memory, contested heritage, and urban redevelopment. As such, the study of Thessaloniki’s Bedesten, caravanserai, and Hamza Bey Camii resonates with broader concerns in the humanities about the intersections of empire, urbanism, and memory.

**Further Reading**

Cezar, M. “Typical Commercial Buildings of the Ottoman Classical Period and the Ottoman Construction.” *International Journal of Middle East Studies* 17, no. 4 (1985): 542–44.

A concise yet influential study outlining the main features of Ottoman commercial architecture, fundamental for understanding urban economic structures.

Eyice, S. “Les Bedestens dans l’architecture turque.” In *Atti del Secondo Congresso Internazionale di Arte Turca, Venezia 26-29 Settembre 1963*, 113–17. Napoli, 1965.

A pioneering examination of bedestens, highlighting their architectural and social role in Ottoman cities.

Hadjitryphonos, Evangelia. “The Urban Image of Thessaloniki in the 15th Century.” In S. Atasoy, ed., *550th Anniversary of the Istanbul University, International Byzantine and Ottoman Symposium (XVth Century), 30-31 May 2003, Istanbul*, 171–95. Istanbul, 2003.

Offers a detailed reconstruction of Thessaloniki’s 15th-century urban landscape, essential for contextualizing its Ottoman transformation.

Lowry, Heath. “The Evrenos Family and the City of Selânik (Thessaloniki): Who Built the Hamza Beğ Câmi’i & Why?” *Occasional Papers in History* 2 (2010): 3–102.

Provides an in-depth historical analysis of the Evrenos family and their patronage, illuminating early Ottoman Thessaloniki.

Texier, Charles, and R. P. Pullan. *Byzantine Architecture*. London, 1864, 130.

A classic 19th century work documenting Byzantine monuments, still valuable for its detailed descriptions and early visual records.
