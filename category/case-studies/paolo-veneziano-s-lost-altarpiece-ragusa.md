*By Christopher Platts | University of Cincinnati*

**Description**

On 18 April 1352, a Ragusan patrician named Nikola Lukarević (d. 1352) dictated his will, stating that he had already paid 50 *hyperpyrons* (about 25 florins) to “Master Paolo, painter of Venice” for an “altarpiece” destined for Lukarević’s funerary chapel in the church of St. Dominic in Ragusa (Dubrovnik). Since 1965, when art historian Grgo Gamulin first suggested that this “Master Paolo, painter of Venice” was Paolo Veneziano, the leading painter in 14th-century Venice, scholars have agreed. However, all have assumed that the altarpiece is lost or was never completed. 

There is, however, a surviving polyptych whose iconography and stylistic evidence suggest that it is the painting Nikola Lukarević – or his cousins Moretto and Nissa, who served as executors of Nikola’s will – commissioned in 1352, or shortly thereafter. Currently in storage at the Harvard Art Museums and rarely discussed in scholarship, this fragmentary altarpiece (92 x 126 centimeters) is attributed to Paolo Veneziano and his workshop, and datable to the early-mid 1350s. From left to right, the work represents Sts. Thomas Aquinas, John the Evangelist, Paul, and Dominic. The central panel, which likely depicted the Virgin and Child, is missing, as is the original frame, which has been replaced by a modern one. Based on Paolo’s other, more complete polyptychs, additional saints probably once flanked the central panel, and these, hypothetically, could have included Lukarević’s name-saint Nicholas, as well as Dubrovnik’s patron saint Blaise. Based on the altarpiece’s incomplete program, which includes the two most important Dominican saints, the work was created for a Dominican church, possibly that of St. Dominic in Ragusa.

Stylistic evidence suggests that the Harvard polyptych was created between 1349 and 1358, and more specifically during the first half of the 1350s, when the Lukarević altarpiece would have been commissioned. Several scholars have rightly argued on stylistic grounds that the figures in the painting – specifically the faces of Sts. John and Paul – are nearly identical to those in Paolo’s Chioggia, Bologna, Louvre, and Sanseverino polyptychs, which are dated or datable to the period between 1349 and 1358. Stylistic evidence, like the iconographic program, thus suggests that the Harvard polyptych could be the lost Lukarević altarpiece.

**Significance**

Whether or not the Harvard polyptych was originally the altarpiece commissioned for the Lukarević family burial chapel in the church of St. Dominic in Ragusa, the documentary evidence of Nikola’s will, is significant, for it provides valuable information about the kind of cross-cultural patronage that Paolo Veneziano attracted in 14th-century Eastern Europe. Investigating Nikola Lukarević’s career, testament, and connections throughout the Adriatic region and the Balkan Peninsula, including links to Venice, Ragusa, Albania, Bosnia, and Serbia, is insightful as a case study. 

Lukarević was an immensely wealthy and well-connected merchant, investor, and lender based in Ragusa during the first half of the 14th century. He primarily put his money into the lucrative salt trade, luxury textiles, and real estate, including residential and commercial buildings such as customs houses. He owned numerous saltworks, which appear to have been the greatest source of his affluence. He also possessed multiple houses for himself and his business partners, including a domicile in Venice. According to his will, once he died, his one-third share of this Venetian dwelling was to be rented out annually for 30 *hyperpyrons*, and that sum given to the Ragusan Dominicans for the upkeep of Lukarević’s private chapel in their church.

Lukarević’s international business network was extensive: he frequently dealt with Venetian, Croatian, Albanian, Bosnian, and Serbian merchants and officials in various cities on both sides of the Adriatic and in the Balkans. His business dealings even extended to the Serbian royal court, specifically to representatives of King Stefan Uroš IV Dušan (r. 1331–55), who granted Lukarević multiple annual commercial contracts in the 1340s. For instance, in 1343 the Ragusan merchant gained a monopoly on salt imports for the entire Serbian kingdom including parts of Albania and Bosnia, and from that point onward Lukarević received special privileges granted by the king himself.

In 1352, after four decades of professional activity, Lukarević was very rich. When he dictated his testament that year, he left 300 *hyperpyrons* to the Ragusan Dominicans so that they would sing 1,000 masses for the salvation of his soul. This was in addition to the 30 *hyperpyrons* the friars were to receive annually for the upkeep of his burial chapel. Lukarević paid for Dominican priests to say one regular mass each day in his chapel and additional masses on the feast of St. Nicholas. Furthermore, he left 200 *hyperpyrons* for an antependium and 30 *hyperpyrons* for a chalice, both for the altar in the chapel. These significant sums would have bought some of the finest liturgical paraphernalia available such as a gilded-silver and enameled chalice and a silk altar frontal perhaps like the one Paolo Veneziano designed two decades earlier for the high altar of Krk (Veglia) cathedral in northern Dalmatia. 

Located immediately to the right of the sanctuary in the east end of Dubrovnik’s largest church, the Lukarević chapel would originally have been extravagantly furnished with these items and with Paolo Veneziano’s altarpiece. Now, however, the chapel is appointed with modern furnishings, including the altar itself, and is spatially delimited by an early modern baldachin.   Lukarević's magnificent visual ensemble would have showcased his religious devotion, wealth, and cultural cosmopolitanism, simultaneously encouraging visitors to the church to pray for him while the Dominicans sang masses for the salvation of his soul. 

Although it is not certain that the lost Lukarević altarpiece can be identified with the Harvard polyptych, the documentary evidence for the patron’s connection to Paolo Veneziano and his successful business career demonstrates the taste for Venetian art in Croatia, as exemplified by a single commissioner who had seen the art and architecture on both sides of the Adriatic.

**Further Reading**

Belamarić, J. “La Dalmazia nella storia della pittura dal Duecento al Quattrocento.” In *Il Trecento Adriatico: Paolo Veneziano e la pittura tra Oriente e Occidente*, edited by F. Flores D’Arcais and G. Gentili. 2002. 32-43.

This book chapter discusses works by Paolo Veneziano commissioned for the church of St. Dominic in Ragusa as well as other late medieval Italian and Croatian paintings for Dalmatian foundations.

Cooper, D. “Gothic Art and the Friars in Late Medieval Croatia: 1212-1460.” In *Croatia: Aspects of Art, Architecture, and Cultural Heritage*, edited by J. Norwich, et al. London, 2009, 76-97.

Focusing on the Franciscans and Dominicans, the author discusses Paolo Veneziano’s commissions for the Dominican church in Ragusa, giving one of the most detailed analyses yet of the church interior and its late medieval furnishings.

Gamulin, G. “Un crocifisso di Maestro Paolo e di altre due del Trecento.” *Arte veneta* 19 (1965). 32-43.

In this work, the author first connected the will of Nikola Lukarević, specifically the altarpiece the testator wished to have in his burial chapel, to the Venetian painter Paolo Veneziano.

Muraro, M. *Paolo da Venezia*. Philadelphia, 1970. 

In this monograph on Paolo Veneziano, Muraro discusses the style and dating of the Harvard altarpiece.

Pedrocco, F. *Paolo Veneziano*. Milan, 2003. 

Pedrocco’s more recent monograph on Paolo Veneziano discusses the painter’s two commissions for the Dominican church in Ragusa, including the Lukarević altarpiece.

Tadic, B. *Građa o slikarskoj školi u Dubrovniku XIII - XVI*, vol 1: 1284-1499. Belgrade, Istorijski Institut, 1952.

This work is the best source on Lukarević’s life and career. It has not been overlooked by art historians and other scholars.
