*By Myroslav Voloshchuk | Vasyl Stefanyk Carpathian National University (Ivano-Frankivsk, Ukraine)*

**Description**

The first written mention of the Virgin Mary church (or later Assumption Cathedral) in the Princely center of ancient Halych (now Krylos village in Halych region) is related to the burial ceremony of Prince Yaroslav Volodymyrkovych (r. ca. 1152/3–1187) from the Rostislavovid dynasty on October 1, 1187 and can be found in the *Kyivan litopys* of the 12th century. But the construction of the temple was initiated earlier, probably during the last years of Yaroslav’s father’s rule, Prince Volodymyrko Volodarevych (r. 1124–1152/3). This is confirmed by the archaeological investigation conducted by Dr. Yaroslav Pasternak during 1936–37, and Dr. Yuriy Lukomskyy during 1992–8. The next reports of the temple are noted in the *Chronicle of the Romanids* (*Galician-Volhynian Chronicle*) of the 13th century. The first mention dates to September 1210/11 on the occasion of enthronement in Halych of Prince Danylo Romanovych (d. 1264) from the Volhynian Mstyslavovid dynasty. The second one relates to spring 1221 during the siege of Halych by Prince Mstyslav Mstyslavovych (d. ca. 1228). The defenders of Halych led by King of Halych and Hungarian Prince Coloman (r. ca. 1214–1221) created the fortress around the Assumption Cathedral and bravely defended it for approximately two weeks. After that Coloman, his bride Salome and a dozen of Hungarian and Polish nobility were taken by Prince Mstyslav, imprisoned or sold to the Cumanians, who supported him in this campaign. The final mention dates back to 1254/5 during the short-term stay of Prince Izyaslav Volodymyrovych from the Olhids dynasty in Halych. 

The Assumption Cathedral was the central church of the Orthodox Halych bishopric from the mid-12th century to 1302/3, and later. The temple fell into disrepair and was destroyed at the end of the 15th century. 

The archaeological remains of the Assumption Cathedral are an architectural monument of national importance and part of the National Reserve “*Ancient Halych*”.

**Significance**

Since the temple fell into disrepair between the 16th century until the first third of the 20th century, the information about its location was unknown. The location of the church was searched for after long-term archaeological investigations led by Dr. Izydor Sharanevych, Lev Lavretskyy and Dr. Joseph Pelenskyy in the second half of the 19th and the beginning of the 20th centuries. Finally, archaeological excavations initiated by the Taras Shevchenko Scientific Society from Lviv, led by Dr. Yaroslav Pasternak, supported and blessed by the Greek-Catholic metropolitan Andrey Sheptycky, revealed the church on July 25, 1936. That sunny day one of the workers, Dmytro Mykhalchuk, blundered upon the massive wall of cutting stone blocks. It was the first track of the foundation of the Assumption Cathedral. The following results of the research showed that the temple was a magnificent four-poled three-apsed construction encircled with galleries from three sides. General measures of the plan are 32,5×37,5 m. It was one of the largest churches of the Kyiv Metropolis of the 12th century. 

The foundation of the Cathedral is made of bricklaying mainly of manufactured blocks of lime and chalky stones. The walls are made of bricklaying hewed blocks of limestone and alabaster. The entrances to the church were decorated with perspective portals, toothed frieze prevailed in external decorations, half-column with caps, and cornices. The archaeological finds at the site also revealed interior decorations. The floor of the Assumption Cathedral was made of tiles of alabaster and sandstone in combination with ceramic tiles, decorated reliefs images of plants and fantastic animals (discovered during archaeological research). Fragments of plaster with the tracks of frescos certify that the church had paintings.

During the archaeological excavation in 1937 a stone sarcophagus with the mortal remains of a male skeleton was found at the entrance of the temple. Dr. Yaroslav Pasternak was sure that he discovered the burial place of Prince Yaroslav Volodymyrkovych – the only ruler of Halych who was buried in the Assumption Cathedral, according to the written sources, in 1187. Later in 1995 Ukrainian scholar Dr. Sergiy Gorbenko researched the skeleton and made an anthropological reconstruction of the head of the discovered man, confirming the version of Dr. Yaroslav Pasternak. But a DNA analysis, provided in Canada in 2016, showed an affiliation of the discovered man to the Y-chromosome haplogroup E1b1b. It means that this buried man probably was born in Northern Africa or the Balkans and could be a bishop of Halych or somebody important among the persons from the clergy. As is well known, metropolitans and bishops in Rus’ were mostly Greeks or Bulgarians. But another skeleton, discovered during archaeological excavation by Dr. Yaroslav Pasternak in 1937, is of a female figure and has undoubtedly been linked to one of the daughters of Prince Yaroslav Volodymyrkovych. The scholars from Lviv made a 3D reconstruction of her face in 2024. 

Medieval sources attest that at the Cathedral there was a library and a workshop for copying  books (scriptorium). The church was in use until the end of the 15th century, later fell into disrepair and was destroyed. The main reason for this was the slow depopulation of the Halych land since the 13th century. The permanent wars caused the migration of the Halych people or part of them died in the battles, struggles, and different conflicts among the neighboring dynasties. As a result, the population number of the community of Halych decreased. The city lost its role as a Princely or Royal capital. The most substantial factor to the complete desolation of the temple was caused by the destruction of the city, mostly by the Tatars and Vlachs, causing the diminishing of the population, which already had no resources to maintain this monumental building. In 1455 the King of Poland Casimir IV (1447–1492) from the Jagiellonian dynasty by special decree forbade the building or repair of  Halych orthodox temples. This decree, focused on supporting Catholics, destroyed the hopes of the Galicians on the renewal of the Assumption Cathedral. From this failure the stone bricks from the Assumption Cathedral partly became the building material for the Assumption church in Krylos village and Halych castle in the 16th century.
