*By YaroslavZatyliuk | National University of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy*

**Description**

The Virgin of Kholm Icon is the oldest among the surviving Byzantine works of art in Ukraine. Researchers traditionally date the icon between the 12th and the early 13th centuries and, based on its technique, associate it with an icon-painting workshop in Constantinople. This dating is grounded in comparisons of the icon with other Hodegetria Dexiokratousa icons of similar style, created during the same period in Sinai (the Monastery of St. Catherine), on Mount Athos (the Vatopedi and Hilandar monasteries) and in Italy. At the same time, it is a revered miracle-working icon in both the Greek Catholic and Orthodox confessions. Up to the 20th century, the icon was the property of the cathedral of Kholm in Poland (Polish Chełm) where its cult developed. Today, the icon is housed in the Museum of Volyn Icon Museum in Lutsk.

The icon is painted on three equal-sized cypress panels joined together. Its actual dimensions are 95.5 × 66.5 cm, and the maximum thickness of the boards is 2.4 cm. The entire surface of the icon was covered with canvas (*pavoloka*). A half-length depiction of the Mother of God of the Hodegetria type Dexiokratousa (“right-hand holding”) variant is placed against a golden background. The defining feature of this iconographic type is the representation of the Christ Child reclining semi-horizontally on the Virgin’s right arm. Christ’s right hand is raised in a gesture of blessing, and it is touched by the left hand of the Mother of God. In his left hand, Christ holds a scroll of the Gospel. His gaze is directed toward the Mother of God, while her head and eyes are inclined toward him. The icon’s color palette is rendered in dark tones. The Virgin’s maphorion (mantle), adorned with a golden star on the forehead, is dark cherry in color with gold bands along the edges. Christ’s garments are dark ochre, with the folds modeled in gold. 

In 1979, the icon was repainted based on a late-19th-century Russian color lithograph of the image. This resulted in losing the original painting in the area of the Virgin’s right eye, on the shoulders, and along the joints where the panels were glued together. Additional losses were caused by various mechanical damages. The icon underwent a complex restoration in the early 2000s to recover its original image. 

A notable feature of the icon is its metal adornments: three rectangular ones with floral decoration (two on the left and one on the wrist of the right hand), and one circular kolt depicting a tree and two birds (on the elbow of the Virgin’s left arm). All these ornaments are executed in the *cloisonné enamel* technique, characteristic of Byzantium and Rus’ in the 11th–13th centuries. They are considered part of the icon’s original adornment, and it is possible that there were more such decorative plaques.                     

**Significance**

The Kholm Mother of God is a unique example of the spread of the Byzantine Hodegetria Dexiokratousa type in Eastern Europe. The development of this iconographic type is documented in the monasteries of Mount Athos, on Sinai, and in Italy from the late 11th through the 13th century. The latest examples date to the early 14th century and originate from the Church of the Virgin Peribleptos in Ohrid. The very origins of the Hodegetria Dexiokratousa image can be traced to the depiction of the Mother of God in the Roman church of Maria Nuova (now Santa Francesca Romana). Its creation was traditionally attributed to the Evangelist Luke.

In terms of artistic quality, the Kholm Mother of God is considered a work of the Constantinopolitan school. It is impossible to determine with certainty the exact time and circumstances of its arrival in Rus’. One can only note that images, dating to the 12th century,  with iconography similar to that of the Kholm Mother of God existed in Kyiv. In particular, a similar manner of depicting Christ on the Virgin’s right arm can be seen in a stone relief discovered at the site of the Church of the Tithes,the primary seat of the Rus’ metropolitans which was destroyed during the Mongol invasion in 1240. A comparable iconography is also found on a 12th-century gold necklace discovered in Bilhorod, near Kyiv. Scholars have also suggested that the precious adornments present on the icon (the kolt and the rectangular plaques) may have been produced in Kyiv.

The question of how and when the icon came to Kholm remains a matter of scholarly debate. Scholars traditionally point to the account in the Galician-Volhynian Chronicle telling that when Prince Danylo Romanovych was building Kholm after 1237, he brought there icons of the Savior and the Mother of God from Kyiv, and adorned them with “precious stones and golden beads.” However, it is unclear whether the image mentioned in the chronicle is the Kholm Mother of God. The same chronicle also reports a fire in the church containing these icons during the Mongol conquest of Rus’. For this reason, some scholars have proposed an alternative hypothesis: that the icon arrived in Kholm directly from Constantinople, possibly due to Prince Danylo's familial ties.

The next important issue was the veneration of the icon as miracle-working by the Greek Catholic Church, which began in the 1620s. This became an important step in strengthening its prestige amid the confessional struggles in the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth following the Union of Brest in 1596. The initiators of the cult of the Kholm Mother of God were the Uniate bishop Meletii Terletskyi (1630–1648) and the rector of the Basilian college, Yakiv Susha (1641–1649).

The collection of documented testimonies of miracles culminated in the official recognition of the icon as miracle-working on 22 July 1646 by a special commission composed of representatives of the Pope, local clergy, and the nobility. In the same year, Yakiv Susha published the work *Phoenix redivivus albo obraz starożytny Chełmski Panny i Matki Przenajświętszej sławy cudownych swoich dzieł ożyły* (“The Phoenix Reborn Thrice, or the Ancient Image of the Lady and Most Holy Mother of Kholm, Revived in the Glory of Its Miraculous Deeds”). Susha connected the Kholm Mother of God with Prince Volodymyr (980–1015), the baptizer of Rus’, and, drawing on authoritative Polish chronicles, described several accounts of its miracles during the Tatar siege of Kholm in the 13th century. This impressive “historical passport” was further expanded to include narratives of a hundred cases of healing.

In the capacity of bishop of Kholm (1652–1687), Susha republished this work twice, each time increasing the number of recorded miracles: 197 in the second edition (1653) and 700 in the third (1684). The latter included an engraving of the Kholm Mother of God by the renowned engraver Oleksandr Tarasevych, which later became the basis for numerous copies of the image in the 18th-19th centuries.

The Kholm Mother of God gained particular popularity during the Cossack Revolution (1648–1657) and the Deluge (1656–1660), when the icon was taken to military campaigns by the Polish army. The first and most celebrated case was its supposed assistance in the victory of the Polish king John II Casimir (1648–1668) over the joint forces of Hetman Bohdan Khmelnytsky and the Crimean Khan Islam III Giray in the Battle of Berestechko in 1651. The icon thus became regarded as a “military” image. At the same time, Susha’s publications described “wounds” on the icon caused by blows from the swords of non-believers. In this way, the Kholm Mother of God was associated with the Częstochowa Icon of the Mother of God—the most venerated shrine among Roman Catholics in the Commonwealth, specifically revered for the supposed divine help during the siege of Jasna Góra near Częstochowa by Swedish forces in 1655.

The icon remained an object of widespread veneration not only among Roman and Greek Catholics of the Commonwealth but also among Orthodox believers from Cossack Ukraine and the Russian Empire. In 1765, at the request of Uniate hierarchs, it was crowned with two golden crowns, sent by Pope Clement XIII, in the presence of Catholic and Uniate clergy and leading representatives of the secular elite.

After Kholm became part of the Russian Empire following the partitions of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Kholm Mother of God became a sacred relic representing the Synodal Orthodox Church after the abolition of the Union with Rome in 1875. Moreover, it was venerated by Russian emperors: Alexander II (r. 1855–1881) made a substantial donation, and his successor Alexander III (r. 1881-1894) undertook a special pilgrimage to the icon with his wife in 1888. The Russian appropriation of the image was marked by the replacement of its precious adornment in 1891 when a new revetment (*riza*) was installed. The latter was produced in the workshop of the Ovchinnikov jewelers in St. Petersburg, replacing an earlier one made in 1661 by the Lublin jeweler Sebastian Nisiewicz at the initiative of Bishop Yakiv Susha.

During the 20th century, the icon was kept in various locations. During World War II, in October 1943, it was solemnly presented in Kholm under the auspices of the prominent Orthodox Archbishop Ilarion (Ivan Ohienko).  The icon was taken from Kholm the following year by a local archpriest and hidden by his daughter, Nadiia Horlytska. The icon was considered lost until 2000, when it was ceremonially transferred to the Volyn Icon Museum in Lutsk. The Kholm Mother of God is a unique example of cultural transfer from Byzantium to Rus’ and of the subsequent adaptation of such artifacts by different religious communities. 

**Further Reading**

Aleksandrovych, Volodymyr. *Холмська ікона Богородиці* [*The Kholm Icon of the Mother of God*]. Lviv: Instytut ukrainoznavstva imeni Ivana Krypʼiakevycha, 2001.

This brief study offers an analysis of the iconography of the Kholm Icon of the Mother of God, identifies its closest analogies, and suggests a time for its creation and the circumstances of its transfer from Constantinople to Kholm.

*Przywrócona pamięci. Ikona Matki Boskiej Chełmskiej: ikonografia – kult – kontekst społeczny* = *Повернута із небуття. Ікона Холмської Богородиці: іконографія – культ – суспільний контекст* [*The Kholm Icon of the Mother of God: Iconography – Cult – Social Context*]. Lublin; Lwów: Wydawnictwo KUL, 2016.

This collection features research by historians on the development of the cult of the Mother of God of Kholm, the circumstances of the founding of Kholm, and the history of the Kholm Uniate and Orthodox eparchies.

*Ікона Холмської Богородиці: дослідження та матеріали* [*The Kholm Icon of the Mother of God: Research and Materials*]. Lutsk: Volynskyi kraieznavchyi muzei, 2010.

This collection brings together articles regarding the iconography, the evolution of the cult, and the restoration of the Kholm Icon of the Mother of God. It features works by prominent art historians, historians, museum studies experts, and restorers, originally published in various editions between 2000 and 2009.

Deluga, Waldemar. “Ikona Matki Boskiej Chełmskiej. Głos w dyskusji badaczy polskich i ukraińskich” \[The Kholm Icon of the Mother of God: A Contribution to the Discussion Between Polish and Ukrainian Researchers]. Український гуманітарний огляд [*Ukrainian Humanitarian Review*] 7 (2002): 116-131.         

The article provides a critical review of various hypotheses regarding the date of the icon's creation and its arrival in Rus'. Another objective of the paper is to present the reasons behind the popularity of the Kholm Mother of God image within the 17th-century Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth.
