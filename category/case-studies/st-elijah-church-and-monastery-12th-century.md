*By Myroslav Voloshchuk | Vasyl Stefanyk Carpathian National University (Ivano-Frankivsk, Ukraine)*

**Description** 

St. Elijah Orthodox church and monastery was located on the hill of “Ubich”, which is separated from Krylos hillfort by the Mozoliv brook, where the high road passes from Halych to Ivano-Frankivsk (this place called now “Garden of Prokalyy”). This complex was built approximately in the 12th century in the Romanesque rotunda style. This style is characterized by semi-circular arches and more massive forms of the walls and was typical for European architecture until the 13th century. The only document that indicates the date of construction of the church is the description of the monastery from  October 10, 1749, written by the consultant of the Order of St. Basil the Great, Alexander Zemkevych, in which 1071 is indicated as the year of foundation of the church. Such an early date has long aroused skepticism among researchers, because Halych became the capital of the Principality only in the first half of the 12th century and we don’t know anything about religions and beliefs of its inhabitants before. From different hypotheses suggested by scholars, St. Prophet Elijah church could be found to be a prolongation of the Cyrillo-Methodian church tradition in the 11th century, opposed (although it is somewhat similar) to the so-called Kyivan Christianity, brought to the Halych land by the princes of Rus’ in the 11th century. According to one hypothesis, the founder of this church and monastery was the famous boyar of Halych Ilya Shchepanovych, mentioned in the *Chronicle of the Romanids* (*Galician-Volhynian chronicle*) at the beginning of the 13th century. He belonged to the so-called pro-Hungarian group of Halych elites. Scholars have dated the available architectural and archaeological materials to the second half of the 12th, the end of the 12th, or the turn of the 12th–13th centuries. 

The foundation of St. Elijah church and monastery is an architectural monument of national importance and part of the National Reserve “*Ancient Halych*”.

**Significance** 

The first excavation of the church was conducted by Lev Lavretskyy and Dr.  Izydor Sharanevych in 1884–7. In 1887, the entire foundation of the church was found. The researchers discovered that it was a rotunda, from the western part of which a rectangular narthex adjoined, and from the eastern front a half-round apse.

In general, the length of the church is 20,3 m and the width is 9,5 m. The foundation platform is 1,1–2,2 m wide. The depth of the structure is different: in the eastern part – 1,3 m, and in the western part – up to 1,7 m. The type of St. Elijah’s rotunda has no analogues in the architecture of Rus’, but is known in Central European countries, for example, the rotundas of St. George in Bohemian Říp or St. Procopius in Polish Strzelno.

During the excavations, researchers found the sculptural details of the temple, fragments of fresco painting, liturgical objects, and remains of a stone sarcophagus. The sarcophagus was destroyed, composed of several hewn and fitted stone slabs. Who could have been buried there – is still unknown. Building peculiarities of this church, its planning and decoration illustrate the Western European influence on the Halych architecture style. 

Between 1909 and 1911, the church of St. Elijah was researched by Dr. Joseph Pelenskyy. The surrounding territory of the monastery was later researched by two local historians Lev Chachkovskyy and Yaroslav Khmilevskyy.

In 1955, the repeated studies conducted by the Soviet expedition led by Dr. Mikhail Karger from Leningrad clarified the plan of the temple, confirmed the simultaneous construction of all its parts, and discovered the fragments of plaster with blue paint, which made it possible to speak of mural decoration of the interior walls. Part of the building material for St. Elijah church and monastery was taken from the Assumption cathedral of Halych destroyed in the 16th–17th centuries. 

As we know from the sources, the church and the monastery were damaged during the war between the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth and the Ottoman Empire allied to the Crimean Khanate in 1673–76. During the Tatars’ invasion, Halych and its surroundings, including the complex of St. Elijah, were plundered. 

At the beginning of the 18th century after the earlier Tatar invasions, bishop Joseph Shumlyanskyy renewed the church and monastery of St. Elijah. He paid for the rebuilding of 600 zloty in 1707. St. Prophet Elijah church and monastery were visited by the author of the so-called First Ukrainian Constitution (“*The Treaties and Resolutions of the Rights and Freedoms of the Zaporozhian Host*”), hetman Pylyp Orlyk (1710-42). In his travel Diary on March 17, 1722, he wrote: “Halych, the former glorious capital city, where Rus’ princes lived, and now – small and wretched town, where for the arrival of gentry on conventions barely could find an apartment… and I, having dinner, drove out already lately, and by the very bad road I could hardly get in for half mile to Saint Elijah monastery, which was not far away from a road and was related to the Krylos monastery at Halych Cathedral. There, in that wretched monastery, I stayed for the night, when we arrived at night, and there were 5 monks who lived working for themselves”. 

Fifty years passed when Halych petty bourgeoisie one time endowed some money for the proceeding of the temple. Unfortunately, the church and the monastery went into disrepair and were destroyed, despite the attempts to preserve it through unification with other small monasteries in the region. The church-monastery complex finally ceased to exist after the religious reform of the Austrian Emperor Joseph II (1765-90) during 1782–5. The monastery was closed in 1783, although a one-year theological seminary was still operating there from 1779 to 1788, where children of different social strata studied. In 1788, there was no one left in the monastery. The building stood empty. At the beginning of the 19th century, the ruins of the monastery were dismantled, and the metropolitan chambers in Krylos were built from the monastery’s hewn stone.

Nowadays in the place of the former temple a symbolic memorable cross is established, and its foundations are kept as museum relics.
