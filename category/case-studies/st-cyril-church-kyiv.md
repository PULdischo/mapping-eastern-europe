*By Maksym Yaremenko | NationalUniversity of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy*

**Description**

St. Cyril Church is one of four surviving examples of religious architecture from the 11th–12th-century in Kyiv, and one of the few medieval Rus’ monuments preserving 12th-century frescoes (approx. 800 m2). It was built in the 12th century on a high plateau in the Dorogozhychi area – the northwestern outskirts of medieval Kyiv. Excavations at the end of the 19th century revealed that the church was built over underground chambers, which were filled in during the 20th century. It is supposed that these underground galleries were used by monks in the 11th–12th centuries before the construction of the St. Cyril Church. According to one assumption, these were caves used as dwellings by monks, like in the Kyiv-Caves Lavra . By the time the St. Cyril Church was built, the monks had long since left and the builders apparently were unaware of these cavities.

The church, measuring 18.4 by 31 meters along its outer perimeter and standing 28 meters tall, was constructed as a cross-domed, six-based, three-nave structure featuring a single hemispherical dome, an altar area with three apses, and minimal decoration. It was built using plinth blocks of various sizes. There are eight types of symbols on the plinths with which the church was built. Among these eight is the trident (tryzub) — the princely symbol. The thickness of the plastered walls reached up to 198 cm on the north side, facing the slope down from the hill on which the church is located. Two unpreserved annexes adjoined the south and north apses, respectively the sacristy and the parabema. The foundations of a possible covered gallery were discovered on the western side of the church. 

The church was illuminated by windows in the drum of the dome and on the facades, positioned in two tiers. The floor was originally paved with slate, brick, and majolica tiles in the main altar. The central nave measures 2.5 times more than each of the side naves. Arcosolium niches were constructed for burials in the narthex and a baptistery that has not survived to this day. Above the narthex are the choirs, accessed by a staircase in the north wall. In the south wall of the southern apse, there is a passage (65 cm wide) with a staircase in a pilaster, rising to a height of nearly 3 m and opening onto an arched exit. Among the various theories regarding the purpose of this enigmatic architectural feature, unknown to other Rus’ churches, is that the passage led to a place where relics were kept or to a place for the prince’s prayer.

Among the church’s frescoes, the iconography features: the Eucharist, the Annunciation, the Assumption, the Nativity of Christ, “The Angel Rolling Up the Heavens,” the Last Judgment (considered one of the oldest depictions of this topic in 12th-century Old Rus’ churches), and others. Graffiti from the 12th and subsequent centuries have also been preserved on the walls. Based on the harmony between the paintings and the architecture, it is assumed that the builders and painters coordinated the church’s design, construction, and decoration. It is likely that the relics of Saint Cyril of Alexandria were kept in the church.

During the early 17th century, the main central altar was reconsecrated in honor of the Holy Trinity, and a side altar dedicated to St. Cyril of Alexandria was installed in the southern apse. The church was renovated during the 15th century, and it took on Baroque forms during the 17th and 18th centuries. In particular, the vaulting, roof, and shape of the central dome were changed. Four side domes were added,all of which took on a pear-shaped form. Pediments with stucco ornamentation were constructed, and some of the windows were bricked up. In the 17th century, the frescoes were partially covered with tempera; in the 18th century, the walls were plastered and whitewashed. 

In the 1880s, under the direction of art historian Adrian Prakhov (1846–1916) with the participation of painter Mikhail Vrubel, as well as Ivan Yizhakevych, Mykola Pymonenko and other representatives of Mykola Murashko’s Kyivan School of Drawing, the ancient murals were reconstructed and some covered with oil paintings. In places where the frescoes had been lost, new oil paintings were created. During the second half of the 20th century, the medieval frescoes were restored. A portrait of Innokentii Monastyrskyi – the hegumen of the Kyiv St. Cyril Monastery in the late 17th century – has been preserved on the southern wall of the church. Scholars consider his portrait one of the finest examples of 17th-century Ukrainian portrait painting.

**Significance**

St. Cyril Church was dedicated to the 5th-century Saint Cyril of Alexandria, a Great Teacher of the Eastern Church. This dedication was unusual for the time the church was constructed. It also included a hagiographic fresco cycle depicting scenes from Saint Cyril’s life in the southern apse, unique in its completeness. This is the first known church in Rus’ dedicated to this saint. There were a total of 16 scenes from the 12th century. Fourteen have survived to this day. However, 6 of the 14 scenes are only fragments, and 8 of the 14 frescoes are in good condition.

Currently, two versions regarding the founders of the church and monastery have been proposed based on the surviving evidence:

1) The church was founded between 1146 and 1179 by Mariia Mstyslavna (†1179), the wife of Kyivan Prince Vsevolod Olgovych of the Chernihiv branch of the Rurikid dynasty. According to Olenka Pevny’s interpretation, the construction of the church in Kyiv coincided with a surge of interest during the 1150s in Constantinople in the teachings of Saint Cyril, linked to theological disputes, and the arrival in Rus’ of a new metropolitan from there, who brought this interest in the saint to Kyiv.

2) The church was founded between 1139 and 1146 by Prince Vsevolod Olgovych himself (†1146) to commemorate his victory in the struggle for the Kyiv princely throne in 1139. In this version, Mariia Mstyslavna is credited with the completion of the church’s frescoes. The cathedral’s dedication was chosen in honor of Prince Vsevolod  Olgovych’s heavenly patron, Saint Cyril. 

The church served as the Olgovych family’s princely necropolis. In addition to Mariia Mstyslavna, her daughter-in-law Mariia Kazymyrivna, daughter of the Prince of Kraków, was buried in St.  Cyril Church in 1179, and in 1194, Kyivan Prince Sviatoslav Vsevolodovych ,Vsevolod Olgovych’s son, was buried there.

St. Cyril Church was built as a fortress-church on an important way leading to the Podil, the Kyivan suburb. It defended the northern approaches to the city. It was surrounded by a moat and a rampart. The church served as the main church (katholikon) of the St. Cyril Monastery. Almost nothing is known about the monastery until the early 17th century. It is supposed that, like the church, it suffered during the Mongol destruction of Kyiv in 1240. However, the church continued to function after 1240, as indicated by 13th–16th-century graffiti on its walls, as well as rare mentions of it in 16th-century written sources. 

The monastery was rebuilt in the early 17th century – at that time, new wooden monastic buildings appeared on its location, and in the 18th century – stone ones. In 1786, the now called Holy Trinity and St. Cyril Monastery was closed as a result of the secularization reform, and its cathedral was eventually converted into a church attached to a hospital for the mentally ill in the early 19th century.

St. Cyril Church  remains to this day a unique witness to medieval Rus՚ culture. In particular, the church՚s frescoes require further study. Today, St. Cyril Church serves as both an active Orthodox church and a museum, part of the “Sofiia Kyivska” National Preserve.
