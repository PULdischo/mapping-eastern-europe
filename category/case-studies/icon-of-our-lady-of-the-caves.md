_By Alice Isabella Sullivan | Tufts University_

**Description**

One of the most significant medieval icons of Rus is the late-13th-century icon known as Our Lady of the Caves (or Our Lady Svenskaya). Painted with tempera on wood ca. 1288, the icon shows the Theotokos (Mother of God) enthroned at the center of the composition, holding the Christ Child on her lap. To either side of the holy duo, against a gold background, St. Anthony and St. Theodosius of Kyiv stand in supplication. They are two holy figures celebrated as the early founders of one of the oldest and most holy sites of Rus: the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra, also known as the Kyivan Caves Monastery. 

This icon was arguably painted at the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra. Later, other copies were made after it. It has also been stipulated that this icon is a copy of an original that no longer survives. It also received a revetment (precious covering) during the time of Tsar Ivan the Terrible (r. 1533-1547).

**Significance**

Founded ca. 1050 in an area of Kyiv overlooking the Dnieper River, the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra served as an important site of spiritual practice, artistic production, and retreat from the world. The complex derives its name – “Pechersk” –  from the word for “caves”/“pechery”, which refers to the labyrinth of underground passages that developed at the site to serve as places of refuge, burials, and now as pilgrimage destinations for thousands of Christians from around the world.

Medieval texts tell us that the monastery was founded by a man named Anthony (b. ca. 983) from Lubech (about 200 km north of Kyiv). He had traveled to a certain “Holy Mount” – either Mount Athos in Greece or the Holy Mountain Monastery at Zymne in Volhynia – and upon his return to Kyiv, wandered the land in search of an ideal retreat for prayer and contemplation. Eventually, this divinely-inspired monk found a spot in a cave atop a mount, overlooking the Dnieper River, some distance from the city center of Kyiv. There, he settled in prayer and devotion, eventually establishing a monastic community modeled on Byzantine practices.

In the icon, St. Anthony is the older of the two figures. He is shown on the right side of the composition, bearded, haloed, with his head covered, and dressed in monastic garb. Either the underside of his red overgarment or a separate epitrachelion (or stole) displays red crosses on a black background, alluding to his monastic and clerical identity. His scroll, still legible, carries the following inscription in Church Slavonic: “I beg you so, my children, let us be abstinent and not show laziness as God assists us.” All those who gazed upon this icon, especially the monks, understood its message. 

St. Theodosius (b. ca. 1008), on the left, was a disciple of St. Anthony and the third abbot of the monastery. He is shown younger and dressed like his spiritual father, although his head is uncovered but still haloed. His scroll also carried an inscription at one point, which was later rewritten, but no longer legible. Perhaps it detailed his contributions to expanding the monastery and sustaining its monastic life.

Theodosius was also of local origin, from the town of Vasilev (about 35 km southwest of Kyiv). According to his _Vita_ (or Life), St. Anthony made him a priest and told him to serve the daily Divine Liturgy. He did so and even established the basic rules of cenobitic monasticism in order to guide properly his brethren. He thus wrote a _Typikon_ that was intended to inform monastic life, modeled on the famous eighth-century _Typikon_ of the Stoudios Monastery in Constantinople. St. Theodosius’s text greatly impacted spiritual life and practice in other local monastic communities and for generations to come. 

Under Theodosius’s leadership and guidance, the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra grew in size, and the community of monks greatly increased in number. In 1062, on a site revealed to him by God, St. Theodosius built a church dedicated to the Theotokos, which was modeled on Byzantine artistic and architectural styles. In time, monastic cells were established around the church, and then the complex received fortifications. The direct contacts with the Byzantine Empire at this time significantly informed the artistic and architectural heritage of this region.  

This church became the principal church of the monastery – the Cathedral of the Dormition – which has had a tumultuous history since the mid 11th century. Between the 13th and 19th centuries, for example, the monument was affected by natural disasters, like fires and the earthquake of 1230, as well as attacks from invaders. The cathedral was later destroyed in November of 1941 during World War II, rebuilt beginning in 1995, and consecrated in 2000. Most recently, the cathedral was damaged during Russia’s drone and missile attacks of mid-June 2026. This recent attack has been the most shocking to an UNESCO World Heritage site central to the early development of Christianity in the region. Already in 2023, the landmark was added to the list of World Heritage in Danger.

Believed to be a miracle-working image, the Our Lady of the Caves icon has healed people from blindness and demonic possessions, in addition to serving as a palladium (protector, especially against enemy attacks).  

This important early icon connected with the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra is now housed in the State Tretyakov Gallery in Moscow. There, at least, it is protected from the current war, which has already damaged the monastery to which it once belonged. The icon’s journey away from Kyiv, likely around the 15th century, is representative of the dispersed cultural heritage of Kyivan Rus especially in Russian collections as a result of military, cultural, and ideological conflicts over the centuries.  

**Further Reading**

Lazarev, Viktor N. _The Russian Icon: From Its Origin to the Sixteenth Century_. Collegeville, 1997.

A foundational art-historical study of icon painting, including a discussion of the Our Lady of Caves Icon, and covering the regions of Kyiv, Novgorod, Vladimir-Suzdal, and Moscow schools. 

_The State Tretyakov Gallery, Collection Catalogue: Old Russian Painting of the 12th–13th Centuries_. Moscow, 2020. 

A catalogue of the State Tretyakov Gallery featuring rare icons from the pre-Mongol era, including the icon of Our Lady of the Caves. 

_The Icon Collection in the Tretyakov Gallery_. Moscow, 2008. 

A catalogue also published in English of the main medieval and early modern icons in the collection of the Tretyakov Gallery. The icon “Our Lady Pecherskaya” is featured on pages 38-41 with several large-scale details of the saints.
