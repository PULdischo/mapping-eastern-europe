_By Martin-Oleksandr Kisly | National University of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy_

**Description**

The Holy Intercession Church (St. Pokrova) in Sutkivtsi is an unparalleled example of the ‘church-fortress’ typology, characterized by a transition from a purely military structure to a dual-purpose sacred space. Unlike later examples built as hybrid structures from the ground up, the Sutkivtsi monument originated as a secular stone fortress, constructed in the early 15th century by Fedir Sutkovetskyi, one of the village’s first owners. Its location was determined by the strategic necessity of the Kuchmanskyi route, a major artery through the Podillian frontier that served a dual purpose: while vital for commerce, it was also a highly vulnerable military corridor frequently used for Tatar raids. The original plan of the fortress is a classic tetraconch: a central square bay surrounded by four semi-circular towers.

In 1476, the fortress was reconstructed and sanctified for liturgical use. This conversion introduced late Gothic architectural elements, most notably the installation of brick rib vaulting (_nervures_) in the lower sanctuary level. The heavy structural load of these vaults and the combat tier above is concentrated on a single, massive square pillar located in the center of the hall. To distinguish its new identity, builders added high Gothic gables above the central square volume. Despite its ecclesiastical transformation, the building never abandoned its defensive utility. The upper floor serves as a functional battle gallery, overhanging the apses and equipped with nearly 100 loopholes and machicolations for defense. This combination of rough limestone masonry and refined Gothic brickwork represents the technical peak of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania’s borderland engineering. Additionally, remnants of fresco painting have survived in the interior.

The monument’s original integrity was significantly altered by later interventions. In the second half of the 18th century, a wooden bell tower was added over the western apse. A major shift occurred in 1903, when the steep medieval shingle roofs were replaced with shallow metal ones, and a central wooden dome was installed in the “Moscow-Synodal” style, which was aesthetically foreign to the original Gothic design. 

**Significance**

The Church of the Holy Intercession in Sutkivtsi is a quintessential material record of life on the Eastern European frontier. Although often viewed by outsiders as a curiosity, it is actually representative of the broader regional tradition of fortified religious architecture in Podillia. While the Podillian frontier is characterized by a variety of fortified religious structures – such as the defensive monastery and fortified synagogue in Sataniv, the Onuphrius church, synagogue, and Bernardine monastery in Husiatyn, and the fortified Roman Catholic church in Lychkivtsi – the monument in Sutkivtsi stands apart. Its significance is rooted in its rare developmental path: rather than being purpose-built as a sacred space with defensive elements, it is one of the few surviving examples of a purely secular bastion that was later converted into a church. This architectural choice was a pragmatic response to the realities of a frontier society where the line between secular defense and spiritual sanctuary was often blurred by the constant threat of incursions.

The 1476 conversion highlights a sophisticated cultural synthesis. The tetraconch footprint – a layout with ancient precedents in Byzantine and Caucasian architecture – was repurposed to accommodate the aesthetic and structural requirements of a Western late Gothic style. The introduction of brick _nervures_ and Gothic gables signals the integration of Podillia into the broader European cultural sphere, even as its geographic position remained at the edge of the known world. This synthesis demonstrates that the frontier was not a cultural void, but a highly porous zone where diverse architectural traditions were adapted to meet the extreme demands of regional security.

The Podillian model of fortified religious architecture is distinct for its structural compactness. In many other regions, a church was protected by a separate ring of walls; at Sutkivtsi, the church itself is the fortification. The technical expertise required to stack a functional combat gallery directly atop a vaulted sanctuary is remarkable. The central limestone pillar, bearing the crushing weight of both the Gothic brickwork and the armed defenders above, is a masterpiece of medieval physics commissioned by the Sutkivskyi family to anchor their landed estate against the instability of the frontier.

Today, the Holy Intercession Church stands as a powerful symbol of regional resilience and architectural ingenuity. Its significance lies in its refusal to conform to a single typology; it is neither purely Gothic nor purely Byzantine, neither strictly a castle nor strictly a church. The preservation of this site is critical for understanding the material history of the frontier.

Future research on this important monument should prioritize comprehensive archaeological surveys of the surrounding site and non-invasive structural scanning of the masonry. Such investigations would provide crucial data to map definitively the timeline of its transition from a purely secular fortress to a dual-purpose sacred space.
