By Yaroslav Zatyliuk | National University of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy

**Description**

Khotyn Fortress is a medieval monument of historical, architectural, and national significance for Ukraine. Due to its relatively good state of preservation, impressive scale (сertain walls reach a height of over 60 m), and location on the high bank of the Dniester River with beautiful scenery, it is currently one of Ukraine’s main tourist attractions. It officially belongs to the “Seven Wonders of Ukraine,” following the results of the 2007 nationwide contest identifying historical and cultural landmarks.

Khotyn Fortress is a complex system of defensive structures formed in several stages between the 14th and 18th centuries. It consists of the castle and structures built in the 1460s–1480s, and a bastion fortress constructed between 1711 and 1718. Around the irregularly planned castle, which covers an area of 0.6 hectares, lie the fortifications of the large bastion fortress spanning 30 hectares.

The castle is oval-shaped, surrounded by walls 8 meters thick, and five massive towers. Adjoining the castle walls are the Commandant’s Palace, a church-chapel, and barracks. The Commandant’s Palace divides the castle courtyard into two sections. The smaller section served as the courtyard of the castle governor (commandant), while the larger one was the soldiers’ courtyard. Separately, in the middle of the courtyard, stands a 62-meter-deep well, covered by a square rotunda (measuring 7x7 m).

The largest of the castle’s five towers is the North Tower. It is rectangular in plan, measuring 18x19 m, four stories high, with three tiers of loopholes; the lower and upper tiers were designed for cannons, and the middle one for rifles. The tower itself effectively served as a final stronghold for the defenders. Among the remaining four towers, the tallest from the outside is the one near the palace, called the West or Commandant’s Tower. Its exterior height is 62.5 m (for comparison, from the courtyard its height is 17 m), it has a diameter of 5 m and a wall thickness of 2 m. The East Tower, located opposite it, is a 6x6 m square and has an exterior height of 55 m (from the courtyard side its height is 17 m). The other two towers, the Southwest (or Blacksmith’s) and the South Gate Tower, were rebuilt in the 18th century. The first of these two towers housed a craft forge, which is why its diameter is the largest, up to 14 m.

The building of the Commandant’s Palace, referred to differently at various times, is a two-story rectangle measuring 17x10.5 m with a height of 14 m, set upon deep cellars. The building includes an open veranda over the stairs leading to the cellar, measuring 12x8 m, built in the 18th century. The palace walls are decorated with red and dark brown brick and white limestone, adding to its impressive appearance. Located next to the palace are the barracks and the chapel church, which form an integrated space. The two-story barracks building, measuring 29x15 m with walls up to 4 m thick, served defensive and residential purposes and is currently used to house museum exhibits. Adjacent to the barracks is a structure, whose second floor houses the chapel church. The church is rectangular, measuring 16x8 meters, and combines Romanesque and Gothic styles. The recessed portal is considered the finest example of Romanesque architectural decoration in Ukraine, while the noteworthy Gothic element is the narthex added in the 1520s–1540s. The church was converted into a mosque (during the last quarter of the 17th century), and in the early 19th century into an Orthodox church dedicated to the Russian saint Alexander Nevsky. Following its restoration in 1986, it was reconsecrated as the Church of Saints Constantine and Helena.

The castle and its structures are surrounded on three sides by the so-called New Bastion Fortress from the early 18th century, with vast dimensions of 250x1200 m. The fortress features 4 fortified gates and 6 artillery bastions. The stone gates are low, rectangular towers with arched gateways topped with tent-shaped roofs. Of all the gates, the Constantinople (or Bender) Gate is the best preserved. One can still observe elements of Ottoman architecture and golden chronograms with Ta'liq lettering, a script variation of Arabic calligraphy, on the walls and entrance gates. In particular, fragments of poems praising the deeds of pashas, the governors of the fortress during the 17th–18th centuries, have been preserved here. There are also emblems of the Janissary military units that served in the fortress. It is believed that during peacetime, the fortress could accommodate a garrison of 20,000, while during wartime, it could house a force of 60,000 troops along with all the provisions and ammunition. 

**Significance**

Throughout its existence, Khotyn Fortress remained the most powerful defensive fortification in Eastern Europe. From the late 15th to the late 18th century, major armies clashed here in confrontations that directly influenced the outcome of significant military campaigns and the broader geopolitical situation. Before examining these, let us clarify the timeline of the castle’s construction and its respective leaders.

Narrative sources contain no specific information regarding the construction period of the first stone citadel that became part of the castle, the area spanning from the Commandant’s Palace to the North Tower, built in the 1460s-1480s. Archaeological research suggests the existence of an urban settlement with wooden fortifications on neighboring hills as early as the 10th-11th centuries. At the same time, scholars often link the construction time of the aforementioned stone citadel to the reign of Danylo and Vasylko Romanovych (1230s–1260s), the rulers of the Galician-Volhynian Principality, to which Khotyn belonged. Compiled in the 1260s-1290s, the Galician-Volhynian Chronicle describes the deeds of these rulers and their successors, and, most importantly, contains reports of these princes erecting defensive structures following the Mongol conquest of Rus in 1238–1241. Simultaneously, the chronicle notes that after 1259 the princes were forced to dismantle their recently constructed fortifications at the demand of Mongol military commanders. However, the compilers of the chronicle make no mention of Khotyn, so the stone citadel in question could have been built either earlier or later.

This citadel gained greater significance after 1359, when Khotyn became part of the Principality of Moldavia. Archaeological explorations have documented its reconstruction and expansion. Further on, during the reign of the Moldavian Voivode Stephen III the Great (r. 1457–1504), who held all the territory between the Dniester and the Bug, the powerful Khotyn castle was constructed. It featured five towers, a governor’s palace, barracks, and a chapel church. All these structures were erected in the 1460s–1480s, turning Khotyn Fortress into the principal northern border outpost of the Principality of Moldavia, and effectively the best-fortified of all nine fortresses in the state. It is possible that the Moldavian ruler considered Khotyn one of his main residences. This is evidenced by his donation of a Tetraevangelion manuscript to the castle church in 1493, which for some time was called the “Princely” church (its original dedication was to the Dormition of the Mother of God). At the same time, scholars draw attention to the unique decoration added to the castle during that period. Its walls and towers were adorned with red brick patterns based on two Christian motifs that served as talismans: nested “Babylon” rectangles, a symbol of wisdom, and stepped pyramids surmounted by crosses, representing “Golgotha” (the rock on which Jesus Christ was crucified).

It was under Stephen III that Khotyn Fortress demonstrated its significance as a defensive structure. In 1476, it withstood a siege by the most powerful army of that era, led by the Ottoman Sultan Mehmed II (r. 1451–1481), the conqueror of Constantinople in 1453. At that time, the castle’s defense was organized by Stephen III’s maternal uncle, pârcălab Vlaicu, who served as the commandant of Khotyn from 1467 to 1481. The next significant event in the history of Khotyn Castle was its siege by the commander-in-chief of the Polish army, Jan Tarnowski, in the summer of 1538. During the two-week siege, the Poles dug mines under the fortress walls and set gunpowder to blow them up. However, the assault was not pursued further, as the then Moldavian Voivode Peter Rareș (r. 1527-1538; 1541-1546) requested peace and swore an oath of vassalage to the Polish King Sigismund I the Old (r. 1506-1548). This act was inevitable, even if the walls of Khotyn had withstood the Polish assault. Indeed, on the eve of the siege, a Polish-Ottoman agreement had been concluded regarding the deposition of Peter Rareș. Nevertheless, under the walls of Khotyn, Rareș managed to reach an agreement with the Polish king, effectively placing himself under a dual protectorate.

Nearly a century later, in August–October 1621, Khotyn castle became the site of a large-scale military clash between the Ottoman Empire and the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth. The ambitious young Sultan Osman II (r. 1618–1622) brought a massive army (estimated by historians at 120,000–150,000) to Khotyn, which was equipped with three hundred cannons. Facing it was an army half its size, composed of Polish soldiers, Lithuanian units, and Zaporozhian Cossacks. Despite this, the walls of Khotyn withstood a months-long assault, and on September 29, both forces concluded an armistice. Thus, the border castle of Khotyn became a barrier to the advance of the most powerful army of that era. Furthermore, under the terms of the truce, Polish forces handed the fortress over to the Moldavian voivode, a protégé of the Ottoman sultan.

Half a century later, Khotyn Fortress once again became the epicenter of another war between the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth and the Ottoman Empire. It was here, on November 11, 1673, that a 30,000-strong Polish-Lithuanian army under the command of Crown Hetman Jan Sobieski, reinforced by Cossack detachments of the Right-Bank Hetman Mykhailo Khanenko, managed to defeat a 40,000-strong Ottoman contingent led by Hussein Pasha. This victory became the subject of various artistic reflections and secured Jan Sobieski’s election as the King of Poland. However, one year later, the fortress was returned to the possession of the Moldavian voivode, a vassal of the Ottoman sultan, and the sultan ordered the demolition of the castles in Neamț, Suceava, and Khotyn. In execution of this order, in 1675, the southern wall of Khotyn Fortress along with two towers were destroyed, most of the southwest tower was demolished, and breaches were made in the western and northwestern parts of the wall, rendering the monument unusable for military purposes.

Following the Russian-Turkish War of 1709–1711, Khotyn became the center of a separate Ottoman administrative district and a key defensive stronghold. Between 1711 and 1718, the old castle was repaired and integrated into a massive fortress designed to accommodate an army of 20,000 to 80,000 men. Built according to the fortification principles of the French engineer Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban (1633–1707), the Khotyn fortress became the finest manifestation of Ottoman military and cultural presence in Ukrainian lands. It housed Janissary barracks and an arsenal, mosques with minarets, palaces for Turkish officials, an imam, and a muezzin, as well as famous Turkish baths and, as contemporaries recalled, a small harem for 30 women. Throughout the 18th century, the Khotyn Fortress was never taken by assault; however, its garrison capitulated during the Russo-Turkish Wars of 1739, 1769, and 1788 (the latter took place on honorable terms, allowing an unhindered evacuation).

Following the signing of the Treaty of Bucharest in 1812, Khotyn Fortress, along with the entire terrain between the rivers Dniester and Prut (present-day Moldova and the southern part of Ukraine’ s Odesa region), became the property of the Russian Empire. A Russian military garrison was stationed at Khotyn Fortress, and repair works were carried out. The Russian imperial presence was marked by the destruction of attributes of 18th-century Ottoman religious structures and the construction of the Church of Saint Alexander Nevsky, a key symbol of the empire. After the Russian defeat in the Crimean War of 1853–1856, Khotyn Fortress ceased to function as a military site and was transferred to the control of the civil administration.

Following the destruction during World War II (in particular, the demolition of the minaret in July 1941), Khotyn Fortress was recognized as a cultural monument in 1963, and restoration work as well as archaeological research have been conducted at the site since the 1980s. In 2000, the castle and fortress became the foundation of “Khotyn Fortress” Historical and Architectural Reserve. Today, this complex of structures is significant as a 14th–18th century monument of fortification and culture where crucial military and political events took place. These structures reflect the impact of various cultural traditions. Since the complex of castle and fortress structures is well-preserved, it serves as a key tourist attraction in the region and an attractive location for filming historical movies. For example, the films _Taras Bulba_ and _The Arrows of Robin Hood_ were shot there.     

**Further Reading**

Mysko, Yulia; Buynovska, Yelyzaveta. _Хотинська фортеця XIII-XIX ст. історія та основні об’єкти_ [_Khotyn Fortress 13th–19th centuries: History and Key Objects_]. Khotyn: Derzhavnyi istoryko-arkhitekturnyi zapovidnyk ‘Khotynska fortetsia’, 2011.

A concise popular-science essay and guidebook prepared by leading researchers of the State Historic and Architectural Reserve ‘Khotyn Fortress’. The book presents the main architectural elements of the Khotyn Fortress, as well as the key events and heroes associated with them.

Mykhailyna, Liubomyr; Pyvovarov, Serhii. _Нариси з історії Хотинської фортеці. Факти, легенди, гіпотези_ [_Essays on the History of Khotyn Fortress: Facts, Legends, Hypotheses_]. Khotyn: Derzhavnyi istoryko-arkhitekturnyi zapovidnyk ‘Khotynska fortetsia’, 2011.

This scientific monograph presents the results of archaeological and numismatic studies on the territory of the Khotyn Fortress and adjacent areas. In connection with this, detailed explanations of changes in the architecture of the main monuments of the fortress are offered. The book also features little-known facts from the history of the fortress and historical personalities who visited Khotyn.

Vecherskyi, Viktor. _Фортеці й замки України_ [_Fortresses and Castles of Ukraine_]. Kyiv: Baltia-Druk, 2011. С. 559–566.

The monograph is a detailed scientific description of the architectural features of defensive structures on the territory of Ukraine. A separate section of the book is devoted to a detailed historical and architectural analysis of the Khotyn Fortress. In it, the author exhaustively reveals the stages of construction and modification of the monument's main structures.
