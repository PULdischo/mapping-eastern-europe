*By Martin-Oleksandr Kisly | HistoryDepartment of National University of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy (NaUKMA)*

**Description**

The Juma-Jami (Friday Mosque) in the city of Yevpatoria (Gözleve or Kezlev) in Crimea is a noteworthy monument of 16th-century architecture, designed by the imperial Ottoman architect Mimar Sinan and commissioned by the ruler of the Crimean Khanate Devlet I Giray (r. 1551–77). Construction began in 1552 and was completed ca. 1564 during the reign of Mehmed II Giray (r. 1577–84). The mosque is a central-domed limestone structure that dominates the urban skyline. Its primary prayer hall is a square covered by a massive 11-meter diameter dome resting on spherical pendentives and four support arches. This central dome is pierced by 16 windows at its base, while the entire drum and arch system contains 40 windows, creating an interior filled with natural light. The hall is flanked by two-story side galleries, each roofed by three flat domes, and a northern entrance portico covered by five domes. Two 30-meter minarets, featuring carved stone balconies and conical caps topped by gilded crescents, flank the entrance.

The 15th-century Tekke of Dervishes, located further inland, is the only intact Sufi monastery surviving in Crimea today. A *tekke* was a specialized spiritual retreat designed for Sufi brotherhoods—mystical ascetics within Islam—to live, study, and perform esoteric rituals. The main building is a square structure measuring 17.5 meters per side, topped by a 9.3-meter dome. The interior features a central hall specifically engineered for the *Sema* (the iconic whirling prayer ceremony) surrounded by 19 small, windowless cells for the resident dervishes. These cells are separated by low-vaulted walls that serve a structural purpose as buttresses for the central dome. Lighting in the main hall is provided by two tiers of pointed windows, while the cells remain dark, illuminated only by tiny slits in the ceiling to emphasize a life of ascetic isolation. The complex expanded over time, later incorporating the 16th-century Shukurla-Efendi mosque. It also contains the ruins of a ritual bathhouse (hammam). Dating to the site's original late medieval phase in the 15th century, this bathhouse provided the essential purification facilities required before prayer and ritual, illustrating how the compound functioned as a self-sustaining spiritual enclave from its inception.

Both the Tekke of Dervishes and the Juma-Jami Mosque exemplify the monumental simplicity characteristic of early Crimean architecture. Rather than relying on the intricate tilework, delicate stucco, or elaborate surface decoration common in later Islamic architectural periods, this style achieves its aesthetic power through sheer structural mass. In both the ascetic Sufi monastery and the grand imperial mosque, the visual focus is strictly on unadorned local limestone masonry and pure geometric volumes—most notably the stark, heavy transitions from cubic structural bases to smooth hemispherical domes. The impact of the architecture is derived entirely from imposing scale, solid proportions, and uncompromising functionalism rather than superficial ornamentation.

**Significance**

The architectural and spiritual landscape of Gözleve represents a critical juncture in the cultural history of the Crimean Khanate. The Juma-Jami is exceptional as the only mosque in Crimea designed by Mimar Sinan, the imperial architect of the Suleymaniye in Constantinople, modern Istanbul. Its central-domed design reflects the 16th-century architectural concept of unity, which served as a visual manifestation of the political and national consolidation of the state under the Giray dynasty. By adopting a design modeled after the Hagia Sophia, Sinan bridged Byzantine structural traditions with Ottoman-Crimean aesthetics. The deliberate use of 40 windows to “detach” the dome from its supports symbolizes the divine and provides a template that later inspired other major Crimean monuments, such as the Mufti-Jami (1623) in Feodosia.

The Juma-Jami Mosque was previously called Khan-Jami – the Khan's mosque. Beyond its architectural form, the Juma-Jami functioned as a state monument essential to the legitimacy of the Khans. It was here that new rulers, arriving from Istanbul, underwent their inauguration ceremonies. The mosque served as the repository for the firman (the Sultan's decree) and the formal act of accession, which every Khan was required to sign before assuming the throne. This legal and spiritual function made the mosque the symbolic heart of the Khanate’s administrative system in Gözleve, which was a primary port and military center.

Located within the same historic urban fabric of Gözleve, the 15th-century Tekke of Dervishes offers a contrasting but complementary view of Crimean Islamic society. Unlike the state-sponsored imperial grandeur of the nearby Juma-Jami Mosque, the Tekke’s austere, unornamented design reflects the ascetic values of the Sufis, who prioritized internal mystical experience over external display. As a center for the Mevlevi order (widely known as the “whirling dervishes”), the monastery functioned as a vital hub for the grassroots Islamization of various social strata. Because these dervishes often adopted a tolerant, syncretic approach toward local traditions, the Tekke became a crucial cultural bridge between the Muslim community and the other religious groups sharing this multi-confessional city. Furthermore, the authority of the Tekke's sheikhs extended well beyond the spiritual realm into the political sphere, as they frequently advised the local nobility and influenced Crimean state policy.

The historical continuity of these sites was abruptly severed following the 1783 annexation of the region by the Russian Empire. The transition into the imperial era brought about a process of material and symbolic erasure. Inscriptions were modified to fit the new administration, as evidenced by the entrance marble frieze at the Juma-Jami, which uses the Christian era date (1552) instead of the traditional Islamic Hijri calendar year (AH 959) that would have authentically marked its Islamic foundation. During the Soviet era, the desecration intensified; the Juma-Jami was converted into a museum while the Tekke was used as a storage facility, resulting in the destruction of its internal ceremonial layout and the loss of its sacred function.

In the contemporary context, these monuments have become battlegrounds for the preservation of indigenous heritage, especially following the 2014 Russian occupation of Crimea. The occupational authorities have initiated “restoration” projects that international scholars  characterize as acts of criminal negligence. At the Juma-Jami, there is a pattern of replacing authentic materials with modern cement and concrete, which compromises the building’s structural and historical integrity. This parallels the pattern seen at the Hansaray in Bakhchysarai, where the aim appears to be the creation of a sanitized, tourist-friendly caricature of the monument rather than the scientific preservation of Crimean Tatar heritage. The Tekke is in a particularly precarious state, with its unique status as the last surviving dervish lodge in Crimea, which makes its survival essential to the study of global Sufism. 

The struggle to preserve the Juma-Jami and the Tekke is not merely an architectural concern; it is a fundamental act of cultural resistance. For the indigenous Crimean Tatar people, these sites are anchors of national memory that testify to a sophisticated past of sovereignty, spiritual diversity, and international architectural exchange. The degradation of these monuments represents a systematic attempt to erase the tangible history of a people who have already endured repeated displacement and exile.

**Further Reading**

Chervonnaya, Svetlana M. *Iskusstvo tatarskogo Kryma* \[The Art of Tatar Crimea]. Moscow: 1994. 

This seminal work provides a comprehensive overview of the artistic and architectural heritage of the Crimean Tatars. It is particularly useful for contextualizing 16th-century mosque architecture within broader Islamic artistic traditions and the cultural identity of the peninsula.

Emirov, R. S. "Tekiye dervishey v Krymu kak otrazheniye istorii sufizma" \[The Tekke of Dervishes in Crimea as a Reflection of the History of Sufism]. *Voprosy dukhovnoy kultury* \[Questions of Spiritual Culture] (2015), pp. 71–73. 

This article investigates the Tekke of Dervishes as a critical historical and architectural monument of Sufism in the region. It explores the socio-religious role of the lodge in integrating different layers of Crimean society through spiritual and inter-confessional dialogue.

Krikun, Yefim V. *Pamyatniki krymskotatarskoy arkhitektury (XIII — XX vv.)* \[Monuments of Crimean Tatar Architecture (13th–20th Centuries)]. Simferopol: Krymuchpedgiz, 1998.

This monograph offers a technical survey of the structural evolution and restoration history of significant Crimean Tatar monuments. It includes valuable architectural analysis of the Juma-Jami and the Tekke of Dervishes, documenting their state before contemporary interventions.
