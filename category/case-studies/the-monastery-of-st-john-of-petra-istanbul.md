<p><em>By Jessica Varsallona | Centre for Byzantine, Ottoman, and Modern Greek Studies &ndash; University of Birmingham</em><br />
<strong>Description</strong><br />
Most of the information about the monastery of St. John of Petra dates to the Middle Byzantine period (843&ndash;1204). However, some legends suggest an early date for its foundation in connection to the Egyptian hermit, Baras, who arrived in Constantinople possibly during the Theodosian era (according to his <em>enkomion</em> written by John Mauropous in the 1070s). Other written sources refer to two subsequent major interventions on the monastery. The first one is John Neusteutes&rsquo; testament. John possibly re-founded the monastery between 1084 and 1095, with the support of Anna Dalassene, the mother of Byzantine Emperor Alexios I (r. 1081&ndash;1118). According to the <em>enkomion</em> of John Neusteutes, which Patriarch Kallistos (1350&ndash;53 and 1355&ndash;63) wrote, we know that the aristocrat John Ioalites enlarged the monastery, perhaps during the mid-12th century.<br />
In the Palaiologan period (1261&ndash;1453), St. John became one of the most famous monasteries of the Byzantine capital. After the fire of 1308, the Serbian King Milutin (r. 1282&ndash;1321) restored it and its hospital. In his letter, dated March 1381, Patriarch Neilos I Kerameos (1380&ndash;1388) stated that St. John of Petra was the most important patriarchal monastery of Constantinople, after St. John of Stoudios and St. George of Mangana. The link between Serbia and the monastery of St. John (established by Milutin) endured until the last years of Byzantium, when the Serbian wife of Emperor Manuel II Palaiologos (r. 1391&ndash;1425), empress Helena Draga&scaron;, made a donation to the monastery and the hospital, which, in the meantime, had also become a vital centre of humanistic culture.</p>

<p><strong>Significance</strong><br />
The existence of a neighbourhood in Istanbul called <em>Kesmekaya</em> &ndash; literally &lsquo;cut rock&rsquo; &ndash; has suggested scholars of Constantinopolitan topography that the area was the Byzantine quarter of <em>Petra </em>(literally &lsquo;stone&rsquo;), where the monastery of St. John lay. In the area of <em>Kesmekaya</em>, several Byzantine structures partially survive or are well documented. The most prominent ones are the ruins of Odalar Camii, Kasım Ağa Mescidi, the cistern of İpek Bodrum, and Kefeli (or Kefevi) Mescidi. Also the Boğdan Sarayı is located in this area.<br />
At Odalar Camii, archaeologists identified two main phases of the building: one possibly dated to the 9th or 10th centuries and the second to the mid-12th century. The damages of the fire of 1203 might have instigated the rebuilding of the upper church of Odalar Camii. Thus, archaeological data is, to a certain extent (e.g. two main phases), compatible with the history of the complex of St. John of Petra known from written sources, although not entirely. Certainly, Odalar Camii was in use during the Palaiologan period. Fragments of mural paintings and sculptural evidence suggest use, and this might strengthen the identification with one of the dependencies of the monastery of St. John of Petra.<br />
Because of its peculiar architectural features, which are not akin to other Constantinopolitan churches, the nearby Kasım Ağa Mescidi has been interpreted as the tower of the monastery of Petra, which was in close connection with the hypothetical <em>katholikon</em> of the monastery, the Odalar Camii. The core of Kasım Ağa Mescidi has been dated to the Palaiologan period, according to its masonry. Contemporary sources, such as Clavijo&rsquo;s account or a Short Chronicle, describe the tower of the monastery of St. John of Petra, its use for the preservation of the most precious relics, and the burials of aristocrats below the bell tower.<br />
In addition, the nearby İpek Bodrum cistern has been analyzed with a focus on its sculptural elements, such as columns and capitals. The dating of the cistern is contemporary with Anna Dalassene&rsquo;s refurbishment of St. John of Petra (1084&ndash;95), which included water management, supporting the identification of Odalar Camii, İpek Bodrum cistern, and Kasım Ağa Mescidi as parts of the complex of St. John of Petra.<br />
Not far from this closely related group of buildings, there is Kefeli Mescidi. The word &lsquo;Kefeli&rsquo; might refer to the Caffa homeland of the previous &lsquo;owners&rsquo; who occupied the Latin church of &lsquo;San Nicola&rsquo; and &lsquo;Santa Maria di Costantinopoli&rsquo; (long identified as Kefeli Mescidi and Odalar Camii respectively). In the 19th century, scholars first identified Kefeli Mescidi as part of the Byzantine &lsquo;Monastery of Manuel&rsquo;, but then they proposed its identification as the church (or a dependency) of St. John of Petra. Based on its rectangular plan and its orientation toward the north, it is difficult to propose that Kefeli Mescidi was a church. More probably, this building, the masonry technique of which suggests a Palaiologan dating, was a monastic refectory (dining hall). It is impossible to determine at the moment which monastery it served. If we accept that the Odalar Camii was the <em>katholikon</em> of St. John of Petra, then Kefeli Mescidi might not be its refectory, since refectories are generally located in close proximity to the main church.<br />
In sum, scholarship has not entirely accepted the identification of Odalar Camii as the <em>katholikon</em> of the monastery of St. John, and Kefeli Mescidi has often been taken into consideration in relation to the identification attempts of St. John of Petra. It is worth noting that the monastery of St. John was very prosperous and sizeable. It probably included, to the south, the area from the Aetios reservoir and, to the north, the actual <em>Kesmekaya</em> area, including both the Odalar Camii and the Kefeli Mescidi. Thus, it is likely that it embraced more than a church within its perimeter. Perhaps, Odalar Camii was one of them, but not necessarily its <em>katholikon</em>. Moreover, in the area of Petra, there were other famous churches, such as St. Mary of Petra, the precise location of which has not yet been identified.<br />
In conclusion, though the identification of the single edifices is still unclear, the buildings of the quarter of Kesmekaya encourage reflections on the articulation of a Byzantine monastery within the city of Constantinople during the late centuries of Byzantium. They also encourage further study into the complicated afterlives of these buildings in the post-Byzantine period.</p>
