*By Christopher Platts | University of Cincinnati*

**Description**

Originally destined for a medieval church in Piran, Slovenia, on the border between central and southeastern Europe, Paolo Veneziano’s Piran polyptych is a bilateral, painted and sculpted devotional object with an unusual design and enigmatic function. Dated by inscription to 1355 and currently displayed in the Museo Sartorio in Trieste, Italy, the work features the enthroned Virgin Mary and the Christ Child at the center flanked by Sts. Mary Magdalene, Nicholas, Mark, and John the Baptist to the left and Sts. John the Evangelist, Blaise, Anthony the Abbot, and Catherine to the right.      

Measuring 152 by 247 centimeters, the work is quite large compared to other Venetian Gothic polyptychs. It has been studied for more than a century, but the scholarly focus has only recently extended beyond the painting’s style, quality, and attribution. In the last two decades, writers have begun to consider its provenance, patronage, and peculiar format – including its once-sculpted reverse, oriented upside-down in relation to the painted front – in an effort to understand the historical and liturgical contexts in which the Piran polyptych could have functioned.

The specific church in Piran for which the painting was made is uncertain. It may have come from the collegiate church of St. George, the most important foundation in the diocese of Koper (Capodistria) after the cathedral of Koper. In 1907, the work was first recorded in one of the small rooms off the sacristy of the church of St. George, but it would be unusual for the church’s titular saint, George, to be absent from an altarpiece with eight saints. Instead, St. John the Baptist takes the place of honor to the Virgin’s right, suggesting that the polyptych could have come from the baptismal church dedicated to the Baptist, which was affiliated with and originally located immediately behind the church of St. George. 

**Significance**

Several scholars have proposed that the painting could have been executed for another church elsewhere in the Adriatic region and that the work only arrived in Piran in the 1800s or early 1900s as a result of the widespread suppression of religious foundations in that period. However, a series of overlooked early and mid-14th century inscriptions on site and papal indulgences directly related to the church of St. George confirms that seven of the eight saints flanking the Virgin and Child in the Piran polyptych were especially honored in this religious community. 

Besides the absence of St. George from the polyptych, the fact that the parish priest at the church of St. George in the years leading up to the work’s completion in 1355 was named George suggests that the work was *not* destined for a church dedicated to St. George. For if it had been, that saint would almost certainly have been represented, considering that he was not only the titular of the church but also the name-saint of the influential parish priest who oversaw the college of canons. Instead, the Piran polyptych was most likely created for the adjacent baptismal church of St. John the Baptist, which in the 14th century housed a single altar dedicated to the Baptist. 

Records from Bishop Agostino Valier’s apostolic visit to the baptistery in 1579 indicate that the church had all the “necessary \[liturgical] furnishings” by that time. This document also suggests a patron for the Piran polyptych: Valier records that a flagellant confraternity dedicated to the Baptist had patronage rights to the baptistery’s one altar and was therefore responsible for furnishing it with the paraphernalia for the Mass, including a painted image. In all likelihood, the confraternity of St. John the Baptist, which was one of the oldest and wealthiest confraternities in Piran, had held such rights to the baptistery’s altar since the early trecento when the group established its seat there by 1331 at the latest. 

The original design and function of the Piran polyptych are also uncertain because only in 2002 did scholars realize that its reverse had been prepared with multiple layers of gesso, extensively gilded, and either completed with carved and polychromed wooden reliefs (that have since been removed) or designed to receive such sculptures (that were never completed). It is important to remember that unlike the few dozen bilateral altarpieces that survive from late medieval Italy in which the composition on each face is right-side up, the Piran polyptych has the painted composition on its front face oriented right-side up and had (or would have had) the sculpted composition on the back oriented upside-down. While some scholars believe that the work functioned as a traditional, one-sided altarpiece for which Paolo Veneziano decided to recycle an older, unfinished or damaged panel without scraping down the gesso and gold leaf on the back, others consider the work to have functioned as a two-sided covering for either a reliquary casket or a reliquary niche in the wall of a church. 

Indeed, it seems most plausible that the Piran polyptych once functioned as a so-called *pala ribaltabile* (“reversible panel”), a rare Venetian or northeastern Italian type of two-sided panel believed to have hung against the wall immediately behind and above an altar so that it covered a relic-containing niche. The panel would have been hinged to a supporting structure on the wall just beneath the niche so that the panel could be flipped down to reveal not only the relics in the niche but also the painted or carved images on the back of the panel itself. Perhaps the best surviving example of a *pala ribaltabile* is Niccolò Semitecolo’s St. Sebastian ensemble (dated 1367), which art historians have hypothetically reconstructed in one possible original location in Padua cathedral. As in the case of Semitecolo’s St. Sebastian ensemble, with Paolo Veneziano’s Piran polyptych, the representations on the back of the panel could have been those of the saints whose relics were preserved in the niche and revealed for display when the panel was flipped down on special liturgical occasions. While this explanation accounts for the upside-down orientation of the lost composition on the reverse of the Piran polyptych, it does not clarify why the reverse is now lacking its wooden reliefs. The assumption here – which applies to at least one other example of a similarly enigmatically designed and now damaged, two-sided polyptych by Paolo Veneziano in Rab, Croatia – is that at some point, the carved reliefs attached to the back of the Piran polyptych were removed, transforming the work into a flattened, one-sided painting that could have served as a traditional altarpiece. 

With these hypotheses about the provenance, patronage, design, and function of the Piran polyptych, one additional thought concerning its reception history is warranted. Paolo Veneziano’s paintings destined for the northern and eastern Adriatic – including the Piran, Rab, Krk, and Dubrovnik polyptychs – spurred the increasingly enthusiastic reception of Venetian Gothic art in Istria, Dalmatia, and inland into Eastern Europe. Not only Venetian painting and sculpture but also precious metalwork, rock-crystal objects, illuminated manuscripts, mosaic projects, and buildings were commissioned and imported for their high quality, recognizable style, and cultural cachet throughout the 14th and 15th centuries.

Guarnieri, C. “Una pala ribaltabile per l’esposizione delle reliquie: le *Storie di Santa Lucia* di Jacobello del Fiore a Fermo.” In *Arte Veneta* 73 (2016). 9-35.

Guarnieri also discusses the typology of the “reversible panel,” including Paolo Veneziano’s Piran polyptych, which may be the earliest extant example.

Muraro, M. *Paolo da Venezia*. Philadelphia, 1970.

Two entries by L. Morozzi in this exhibition catalogue treat the Piran polyptych in detail, including its style, attribution, dating, and provenance. The author believes that the artist re-used an older panel, which would explain why the reverse is partly gilded and scraped.
