*By Martin-Oleksandr Kisly | HistoryDepartment of National University of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy (NaUKMA)*

**Description**

The Bakhchysarai Historical and Cultural Reserve is a comprehensive museum complex in Crimea dedicated to preserving the diverse architectural and cultural heritage of the region, most notably that of the Crimean Tatars and the indigenous Karaite community. This case study explores the reserve's two most significant monuments – the valley-based Khan’s Palace (Hansaray) and the adjacent plateau fortress of Chufut-Kale – illustrating the peninsula’s unique evolution of political power, urban development, and defensive topography. 

At the heart of the reserve lies the The Khan’s Palace, a 16th century complex that served as the sovereign seat of the Crimean Khanate. Emerging from the fragmentation of the Golden Horde in the mid-15th century and enduring until its annexation by the Russian Empire in 1783, the Khanate was a major geopolitical power whose territory encompassed the Crimean Peninsula and the vast Pontic-Caspian steppes along the northern Black Sea coast. Established primarily under Sahib I Giray (r. 1532–51), the palace was conceived as a “Garden Palace”, reflecting the Islamic concept of paradise through the integration of architecture, courtyards, and flowing water. The site encompasses the Great Khan Mosque (ca. 1532), the Divan (Council Chamber), and the Harem.

Constructed from local limestone and carved wood, the palace reflects a dynamic evolution of architectural styles over several centuries. A significant early element is the Demir-Qapi portal (1503), which was originally part of the older Devlet Saray residence in the neighboring settlement of Salaçiq. This portal, which features distinct Italian Renaissance stonework, serves as a material nod to the Khanate’s early diplomatic and trade relations with Italian merchant republics like Genoa. It was later incorporated into the Hansaray when the capital was established at Bakhchysarai under Sahib I Giray. The complex continued to evolve through the personal patronage of later rulers, such as the 18th-century Khan Qirim Giray (r. 1758–64), who commissioned the iconic Fountain of Tears (Selsebil) to commemorate the memory of his deceased wife. Although the palace suffered extensive damage during a 1736 fire caused by invading Russian troops, subsequent reconstructions throughout the 18th century maintained its role as the administrative center of the Crimean Khanate until the 1783 annexation by the Russian Empire. It remains the only extant palace of the Crimean Khans, serving as a vital monument to Crimean Tatar statehood and indigenous architectural ingenuity.

Bakhchysarai also contains the plateau fortress of Chufut-Kale, dating to the 13th century. It originated as Qırq Or (Forty Fortifications), a strategic Golden Horde outpost, which explains the presence of the 15th century mausoleum (türbe) of Canike Hanim (d. 1437), the daughter of Khan Tokhtamysh. The site is an exceptional example of rock-cut architecture on a limestone plateau, featuring dwellings and narrow streets carved directly into the rock. After the Crimean Khans established their new capital in the valley below, the fortress became a spiritual and defensive sanctuary for the Karaites, an indigenous people of Crimea who practice a distinct form of Judaism. It is precisely because of this Karaite community – whose heritage is evidenced by two well-preserved kenesas (synagogues) – that the site later became known as “Chufut-Kale,” which translates to “Jewish Fortress” in Crimean Tatar.

Today, the reserve preserves a dense collection of surviving monuments across these two distinct zones. In the valley below, the Hansaray complex is organized around a series of functional courtyards. Its surviving structures include the Great Khan Mosque (Büyük Han Cami) with its iconic twin minarets, the Divan (Council Chamber) where state affairs were conducted, the secluded Harem, the towering Falcon Tower used for observation, as well as the aforementioned Demir-Qapi portal and Fountain of Tears. High on the plateau, Chufut-Kale retains significant physical remnants of its layered urban history. The site is far more than just a ruined settlement; visitors can still walk through its formidable defensive walls and the imposing South and East gates (Kichik-Kapu and Orta-Kapu) that controlled access to the promontory. Beyond the two Karaite kenesas and the 15th century mausoleum of Canike Hanim, the plateau preserves an extensive network of multi-tiered artificial caves and rock-cut utility chambers used for storage and defense. The settlement also features the deeply rutted remains of paved streets, ruins of stone residential estates, and a hidden siege well (Tyk-Kuyu), illustrating the highly developed infrastructure that once sustained thousands of residents on the isolated rock.

**Significance**

The historical trajectory from the fortress of Qırq Or to the development of the Hansaray illustrates a significant evolution in the cultural and political landscape of the Black Sea region. Together, these sites represent the maturation of the Crimean Khanate from its Golden Horde roots into a sophisticated early modern power. The presence of the 15th century mausoleum of Canike Hanim at the plateau fortress serves as a vital material link to the Chingisid lineage, while the subsequent establishment of the “Garden Palace” in the valley floor reflects a shift toward an administrative center that synthesized Ottoman, Seljuk, and local architectural traditions. 

Central to the significance of the Bakhchysarai Historical and Cultural Reserve is its role as a primary heritage site for two indigenous groups: the Crimean Tatars and the Karaites. While the Hansaray functioned as the sovereign heart of the state, Chufut-Kale highlights the religious and social pluralism of the Khanate. The long-term residence of the Karaite community on the plateau, where they maintained cultural and legal autonomy, provides a rare example of multi-confessional coexistence within a shared defensive and urban environment. This enduring presence underscores that the Bakhchysarai region was a continuously inhabited hub of indigenous governance and religious life for centuries.

The Hansaray’s role as a site of indigenous political agency was revitalized during the 1917–18 revolution. In December 1917, the palace served as the venue for the first Kurultai (National Assembly) of the Crimean Tatar people, where the Crimean People`s Republic was proclaimed. This event transformed the complex from a relic of the Khanate into a powerful symbol of modern national self-determination, further cementing its status as the spiritual and political center of the nation during a brief window of reclaimed sovereignty.

From a methodological perspective, these monuments offer a critical study in architectural authenticity and the impact of imperial historiography. Following the 1736 fire caused by invading Russian troops, the palace required extensive rebuilding by the reigning Khans. However, the most profound physical alterations occurred after the 1783 annexation, through a series of imperial “restorations” throughout the 19th century. These interventions were devastating in their extent: the complex was reduced to roughly a third of its original footprint as Russian authorities demolished entire structures, including the Winter Palace, three of the four Harem buildings, and several bathhouse complexes. Within the surviving structures, authentic Crimean Tatar and Ottoman-influenced features were replaced by romanticized, orientalized designs catered to the tastes of visiting Russian royals. Original delicate frescoes and carved woodwork were routinely painted over, stripped, or replaced with European-style stucco, gaudy paints, and even wallpaper meant to mimic an exotic “Eastern” aesthetic. These interventions reflected external imperial fantasies rather than the indigenous intent of the original builders, creating a layer of material erasure that challenges contemporary researchers attempting to recover the site's authentic architectural voice.

The Hansaray is exceptional as the only extant palace of the Crimean Khans, making it a singular monument to a lost administrative tradition. While it shares a common architectural vocabulary with the Topkapı Palace in Istanbul – specifically in its use of “Paradise Garden” layouts – it remains distinct in its adaptation to the specific limestone topography of the Crimean interior. Similarly, the rock-cut urbanism of Chufut-Kale can be compared to the “cave cities” of Cappadocia or the Caucasus, yet it is unique for its continuous function as a sanctuary for the Karaite community well into the modern era. 

The contemporary status of the Hansaray is defined by a struggle for preservation following the 2014 occupation of Crimea by the Russian Federation. Since 2017, the site has been subjected to a massive “restoration” project conducted by occupational authorities that has drawn international condemnation from UNESCO and the global academic community. These interventions have involved the removal of original hand-made Tatar clay tiles, the replacement of authentic oak beams with contemporary pine replicas, and the installation of a heavy metal canopy over the Great Khan Mosque. Such practices effectively erase the physical evidence of the Khanate’s sophisticated architectural heritage and indigenous craftsmanship.

Ultimately, the preservation of the Hansaray and Chufut-Kale is essential for understanding the rooted knowledges of the peninsula. These sites are not merely ruins of a former state but active anchors of identity for indigenous peoples who have faced repeated displacement. By analyzing the plateau and the valley as a single, interconnected cultural landscape, we move beyond colonial narratives of discovery and instead recognize a sophisticated, long-standing tradition of sovereignty and cultural resilience in Eastern Europe. 

**Further Reading**

Gertsen, Aleksandr G., and Yurii M. Mogarichev. *Krepost' dragotsennostey. Kyrk-Or. Chufut Kale* \[The Fortress of Jewels: Qırq Or, Chufut-Kale]. Simferopol: 1993. 

This study is a primary scholarly source for the history and archaeology of the plateau fortress. It traces the site's evolution from the medieval stronghold of Qırq Or to its later identity as a Karaite sanctuary.

Haivoronski, Oleksa. *Poveliteli dvukh materikov. Tom 1: Krymskiye khany XV–XVI stoletiy i bor'ba za nasledstvo Velikoy Ordy* \[The Rulers of the Two Continents, Vol. 1: The Crimean Khans of the 15th and 16th Centuries and the Struggle for the Heritage of the Great Horde]. Kyiv: Maisternia knyhy; Bakhchysarai: Bakhchysarai Historical & Cultural State Preserve, 2010. 

This work provides an extensive political history of the early Crimean Khanate. It contextualizes the development of Bakhchysarai within the broader dynastic competition that followed the fragmentation of the Golden Horde.

Ibragimova, Aliye. *Bakhchisarayskiy Khanskiy dvorets XVI–XVIII vv.* \[Bakhchysarai Khan's Palace of the 16th–18th Centuries]. Simferopol, 2013. 

This monograph offers a specialized architectural study of the palace during its most active period of governance. It is a critical resource for understanding the material culture and spatial organization of the complex.

Kançal-Ferrari, Nicole. *Kırım’dan kalan Miras: Hansaray* \[Heritage of Crimea: The Khan’s Palace]. Istanbul: Klasik Publications, 2005. 

Published in Turkish, this book provides the most complete architectural and historical description of the Hansaray. It is an essential resource for documenting the site's structural evolution and its unique synthesis of Ottoman and indigenous styles.

Kizilov, Mikhail. "The Karaite Communities of Chufut-Kale and Mangup: History and Topography of the Settlements." In *Karaite Judaism: A Guide to Its History and Literary Sources*, edited by Meira Polliack, 755–788. Leiden: Brill, 2003. 

This English-language study provides a thorough historical and topographical analysis of Chufut-Kale. It is crucial for understanding the social autonomy and distinct identity of the indigenous Karaite community within the broader Crimean landscape.
