_By Ana Ionescu | Tufts University_

**Description**

The Mihai-Vodă church, located in Bucharest, Romania, was completed in 1594 and has survived through the Communist period. The church and its monastic complex were built by Michael the Brave (1593–1601), one of the greatest figures in Romanian history. He was the first leader to attempt to unite the Romanian Principalities in 1600. The church served various functions throughout the centuries and today lies hidden behind communist-style flats, steps from the prominent House of the People, yet few notice it. 

The main construction phase of the Mihai-Vodă church took place between 1589 and 1591. Some of the craftsmen who worked on the project came directly from workshops in Constantinople and the Crimea. Michael the Brave dedicated it to Saint Nicholas in 1593, and it was consecrated in 1594, as the inscription close to the entryway indicates.     

The church has a triconch plan with lateral semicircular apses extending north and south, a bema where the altar stands, a prothesis and a diakonikon, and a pronaos extending toward the west. The church has a central main dome and two smaller domes on either side of the altar area. The main western entryway is small, with a Byzantine-style mosaic of Saint Nicholas above it and minimal decoration.

The interior of the Mihai-Vodă church is predominantly decorated with Byzantine-style murals that have been restored over the years. The murals depict various scenes from the life of Christ over a dark blue background. The main dome interior features Christ Pantocrator, while the dome above the altar depicts the Virgin holding Christ Child. The church entrance interior features various frescoes of donor portraits, including Michael the Brave and his wife, as well as the later addition of King Ferdinand I (1914–1927). On the right side of the wooden, gilded iconostasis, a group of five military saints, indicated by their armor, points to the church’s later function as the first military chapel in 20th century Romania. Overall, the interior frescoes and dim lighting create an ephemeral experience for visitors, deepening one’s spiritual connection to the space.

**Significance**

The Mihai Voda Church shared architectural design features with monuments south of the Danube River, indicating shared heritage and cultural exchange throughout the late medieval and post-Byzantine period. The translocal base approach, which draws on exchanges between the Carpathian territories and neighboring regions, allows comparisons between the Mihai-Vodă Church and the Church of St. Stephen or Lazarica at Kruševac, Serbia, illustrating cross-cultural architectural developments throughout time. Prince Lazar (1371–1389) built St. Stephen in the late 1370s; it has a main naos with lateral apses and a tall dome topped with a metal cross like Mihai-Vodă. The exteriors of the two churches are similar in their emphasis on brickwork and the rounded arches. Another similarity is the tall, slim windows on the dome structure that block large amounts of light. This choice preserves the dimly lit atmosphere of Byzantine churches while emphasizing the spiritual, performative journey visitors are invited to take during the liturgy. 

Another comparison can be drawn south of the Danube between the Mihai-Vodă church and the Church of St. John Aleitourgetos in Nesebar, Bulgaria. It dates to the mid-14th century and is said to resemble the architecture of Constantinople. The Church of St. John Aleitourgetos is distinctive for its colorful brick-and-stone exterior decoration; however, differences in their architectural plans are also evident. Despite the temporal difference between those monuments, they illustrate the church-building traditions of Wallachia, Serbia, and Bulgaria. Comparisons between the medieval Carpathian and Balkan regions are essential, as many scholars of Balkan medieval architecture overlook connections with the Romanian Principalities, treating the Danube River as a natural border. This perspective deepens our understanding of a shared visual language in the late medieval period, highlighting visual connections among Wallachia, Serbia, and Bulgaria during the late medieval period.

In 1977, the Mihai-Vodă church, alongside other sites, suffered imminent threats of demolition due to Communist urban planning projects, and the destruction of numerous buildings in Bucharest during the earthquake that year. The Mihai-Vodă church was among those slated for demolition because it stood in a central area of Arsenal Hill, where Romania’s dictator Nicolae Ceaușescu centered his architectural plan. It is unclear under what circumstances, but it was decided to move the church rather than destroy it. The displacement of the Mihai-Vodă church was possible with the help of Eugeniu Iordăchescu. He invented a system for moving buildings along tram tracks over short distances. Through this system, the church was moved 241.9 m and is now hidden behind communist blocks. The church building suffered no damage, but the relocation destroyed the monastic complex.

The church was not destroyed because of its importance in the history of a unified Romanian nation as a site of memory. Given Michael the Brave's role in temporarily unifying the principalities of Romania, this church, as one of the voievode's earliest constructions, has been imbued with proto-national meaning. Therefore, if it had been fully destroyed, the image of the Romanian nation could have been considered under threat, which could have led to increased political unrest. As the integrity of the original place was destroyed through the demolition of the monastic complex and movement from the original location, a new site of memory – or sufferance – was created. The memory of a foundation as strong as the Romanian proto-nationality of the 1600s was then, in the 1980s, relocated by the Communist regime.

**Further Reading**

Ousterhout, Robert G. _Eastern Medieval Architecture: The Building Traditions of Byzantium and Neighboring Lands_. Oxford University Press, 2019.

Chapter twenty-six explores the cultural exchanges between Serbia, Bulgaria, and Romania. It is one of the few comprehensive medieval books that presents the connection between the Carpathian and Balkan regions.

Preda, Caterina. _Art and Politics under Modern Dictatorships_. Springer International Publishing, 2017. 

This book explores the impact of the communist dictatorship in Romania and the Chilean military dictatorship. Chapters four and six, in particular, explore the role of art in Communist propaganda. Those chapters offer a more in-depth view of the 1977 moment and Ceaușescu's architectural project.

Sas, Maria Alexandra. “Arhivele statului în șantier continuu. Cazul Ansamblului monastic Mihai-Vodă din București (sec. XIX–XX).” _Buletinul Cercurilor Științifice Studențești_ 27, no. 1 (2021): 67–104.

This article analyzes the monastic ensemble of Mihai-Vodă in Bucharest as an archival space for the general directory of state archives between 1866 and 1985. It explores the history of the church, its construction, and reconstruction.

Theodorescu, Răzvan, Marius Porumb, and Academia Română (1990– ), eds. _Arta din România: din preistorie în contemporaneitate_. Civilizația românească 10. Editura Academiei Române, 2018.

This book, which provides an overview of Romanian art developments from ancient to contemporary times, offers a unique understanding of the Mihai-Vodă church through the architectural theory of Nicolae Ghika-Budești of the _Old Romanian church style_ (see pages 289–302).
