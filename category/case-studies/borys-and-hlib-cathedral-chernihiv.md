_By Yaroslav Zatyliuk | National University of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy_

**Description**

The Borys and Hlib Cathedral in Chernihiv is a 12th-century architectural monument of national significance. Throughout its more than 900 years of existence, it served first as the private church and burial place of the princes of Chernihiv, later as a monastic church and the cathedral (seat) of the archbishops and bishops of Chernihiv, as well as their burial place. Today, the building functions as a museum with a permanent exhibition.

The cathedral is located in the northern part of the historic center of Chernihiv, within the Dytynets fortress. It was built on the site of the princely court, adjacent to the princely palaces on one side and, on the other, to the city’s largest church, the Spaso-Preobrazhenskyi Cathedral (built in the 1030s by Mstyslav, son of Prince Volodymyr, the Baptizer of Rus', r. 1024-1036). On the northern side of the cathedral, at a distance of several dozen meters, stood the northern rampart of the fortress. Thus, the building occupied a relatively confined area. At the beginning of the 18th century, the Chernihiv Collegium was built next to it.

The present appearance of the Borys and Hlib Cathedral is the result of restoration in the 1950s, which involved a complete rebuilding based on reconstructions of its original form. Prior to that, the cathedral had undergone several episodes of destruction and rebuilding, the most significant of which were its remodeling in the 1630s to resemble a Roman Catholic church, as well as the addition of several vestibules and other structures during the second half of the 17th century and throughout the 19th century. 

The Borys and Hlib Cathedral is a typical example of 12th-century architecture in Chernihiv. It is cross-shaped, six-columned, and crowned with a single dome (6 m heigh). All facades of the cathedral are topped with reconstructed zakomaras (semicircular arches crowning an exterior wall that correspond to the inner vaults). The cathedral is 26.5 m long, 18.5 m wide, and 25 m high. On the northern and western sides, it had galleries, while on the southern side, there was a chapel and a vestibule in front of the portal. These structures have not been reconstructed; only their foundations with surviving masonry have been preserved. Today, they encircle the cathedral. The cathedral has all been plastered on the outside.

The Borys and Hlib Cathedral represents a new architectural trend that emerged in Chernihiv at the turn of the 12th century. It is characterized by a combination of the traditional Byzantine church plan with constructive elements of European Romanesque architecture. In particular, the influence of the latter is evident in the structural design of the cathedral: all the bays of its facades were finished with semicircular zakomaras. Another adapted element is the decorative ornamentation featuring zoomorphic motifs, which are found in the carved capitals with animal figures, as well as in stones depicting fantastic creatures – the Sirin bird, a dragon, stylized lions, and others.

The Borys and Hlib Cathedral is a vivid example of the so-called zakomara-type church, which in the 12th century was used for ecclesiastical constructions in various towns of Rus’ – in Kyiv, Kaniv (the St George Cathedral of 1144), Volhynia, Smolensk, and Ryazan'. Compared to Kyiv churches of this type (the Dormition Cathedral of the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra and the St Michael’s Golden-Domed Cathedral), the Chernihiv Borys and Hlib Cathedral employs a different masonry technique – regularly called ‘opus isodos’. In contrast, the Kyiv cathedrals reflect a more Byzantine tradition of mixed masonry, ‘opus mixtum’ (a combination of plinthos and stone) and a system of concealed courses.

A key feature of the interior of the original Borys and Hlib Cathedral was the arrangement of deep arcosolia niches in the western part of the church. Such niches were used for burials initially for the princes of Chernihiv.

Among all preserved ecclesiastical buildings from the Rus’ period in Ukraine, only the Borys and Hlib Cathedral has retained the omphalion traditional for Byzantine churches. It is a square (measuring 2.9 × 2.9 m) made of two flat slabs, decorated with winding bands of smalt. It is located in the center of the church, beneath its single main dome.

The cathedral was arranged in this form during 1952–1958 according to the design of architect Mykola Kholostenko. The reconstruction involved the demolition of all later additions to the cathedral made in the 17th and 19th centuries.

Today, the cathedral functions as a museum under the authority of the National Architectural and Historical Reserve “Ancient Chernihiv,” featuring a permanent exhibition that highlights the structure’s history and the figures associated with it. It is also frequently used for concerts of sacred music. In December 2025, the Borys and Hlib Cathedral in Chernihiv was included on UNESCO’s International List of Cultural Property under Enhanced Protection.

**Significance**

Textual sources do not provide the date of construction or the name of the founding prince of this important monument. Only in the entry under the year 1123 does the _Kyiv Chronicle_ (compiled at the end of the 12th century) mention the death and burial in this cathedral of the Chernihiv prince David Sviatoslavych (r. 1097-1123). Researchers believe that he was the one who built the cathedral. It is no coincidence that all known male-line descendants of David are buried here.

Importantly, the church was dedicated to the holy princes Borys and Hlib – the first Christian saints of Rus’, sons of its baptizer, Prince Volodymyr (r. 980-1015). They are venerated for their martyr’s death in a fratricidal war over the Kyiv throne. The period from the 11th to the early 12th century was key for the formation of their cult and, presumably, their canonization. Their relics were kept in the church built in their honour in Vyshhorod. After its restoration in 1115 by the grandsons of Prince Yaroslav the Wise (r. 1019-1054), the Chernihiv prince David Sviatoslavych effectively continued this tradition of veneration. This is especially significant given that this ruler was baptized with the name of Saint Hlib. His relatives on the Kyiv throne – Vsevolod Yaroslavych and his son Volodymyr Monomakh – considered Saint Borys their patron. Thus, the Borys and Hlib Cathedral in Chernihiv became part of the practices that developed a dynastic cult of Saint Borys and Hlib shared by the rulers of Rus'. It can, therefore, be assumed that the church was built between 1115 and 1123.

According to archaeological research, the Borys and Hlib Cathedral was built on the princely court, and prior to its construction a two-chamber princely tower was demolished. Nearby, a residence for the bishop was erected.

The Romanesque influences noted above are significant features of the cathedral. Such a synthesis of Romanesque and Byzantine architectural traditions is characteristic in regions of the Balkans, in particular of the Raška architectural school in Serbia during the second half of the 12th century. It may be assumed that the Borys and Hlib Cathedral in Chernihiv was built by invited masters from northern Italy, or by Byzantine masters with work experience in Italy, or possibly some with a Balkan connection. In any case, this monument in Chernihiv is an important testimony to the cultural transfer to Rus' of various traditions.

It is generally accepted that after the burial in the cathedral of the last male descendants of David Sviatoslavych, the Grand Prince Iziaslav Davydovych in 1161 and Sviatoslav Volodymyrovych in 1167, the cathedral ceased to serve as the princely burial place. The princely burials have not survived, but textual sources and some fragments of princely sarcophagi offer relevant evidence. Other princes of Chernihiv began to be buried again in the Spasky Cathedral. Instead, the church became the foundation of a monastery that functioned for several centuries. Scholars explain this by the fact that the Borys and Hlib Cathedral was built as a burial vault for Davyd Sviatoslavych and his descendants, whereas all other rulers of Chernihiv were traditionally buried in the Spasky Cathedral.

After the Mongol conquest of Chernihiv in 1239, the church was severely damaged. There is evidence that its roof was repaired at the end of the 13th century, but almost no information survives about the functioning of the monastery and the church during the period when Chernihiv was part of Muscovy. After the city became part of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth in 1618, the Borys and Hlib Cathedral was entrusted to the Dominican Order. In the 1630s and 1640s, the Dominicans rebuilt the cathedral into a Roman Catholic church. In particular, its three apses were reconstructed as a single three-sided structure, while all the cathedral’s windows were reshaped into elongated pointed arches characteristic of Gothic architecture. It has been suggested that, after its conversion into a Roman Catholic church, the cathedral served as the venue for the _sejmyk_s (regional noble diets) of the Chernihiv nobility. In addition, the cathedral became a burial place for its lay patrons. Archaeologists, for example, discovered a large crypt in the central apse containing the remains of three adults and three children.

After the Cossack Revolution and the establishment of the Zaporozhian Host, the Borys and Hlib Cathedral was returned to the Orthodox Church, and in 1672, the monastery attached to it was re-established. Moreover, it became the residence of the bishops of Chernihiv. In the 1670s and 1680s, Archbishop Lazar Baranovych of Chernihiv, followed by his successor Ioann Maksymovych (1697–1711), undertook a major reconstruction of the cathedral. Extensive stone construction was also carried out around it. This work was financially supported by the Ukrainian Hetman Ivan Mazepa (1687–1709). As a result, a two-tiered octagonal refectory, 21 m high, was added to the western wall of the cathedral. From the east, a new dome was constructed. Consequently, the church became a three-domed structure and fully acquired the characteristics of Ukrainian Baroque architecture. 

At the same time, the interior of the cathedral was also transformed. In particular, it was furnished with an elaborate iconostasis featuring magnificent silver holy gates. The latter were commissioned by Hetman Ivan Mazepa and crafted in Augsburg in 1702 by the renowned jeweler Philipp Jakob IV Drentwett. These exquisite gates form the centerpiece of the cathedral’s present museum exhibition. They measure 2.66 × 1.21 m and weigh 56 kg.

The monastery precinct was further developed. In particular, in 1702, a stone monastery bell tower with the Church of St. John the Baptist (the patron saint of Hetman Ivan Mazepa) was erected beside the cathedral. A two-story refectory with the Church of All Saints was added to the eastern side of the bell tower. This building became the home of the Chernihiv Collegium, which was founded at the same time.

The late 17th and 18th centuries marked the period when the Borys and Hlib Cathedral became the burial place of the hierarchs of the Orthodox see of Chernihiv. Five bishops of Chernihiv were buried here: Lazar Baranovych (1657–1693), Feodosii Uhlytskyi (1692–1696), Amvrosii Dubnevych (1742–1751), Kyrylo Liashevetskyi (1761–1770), and Feofil Ihnatovych (1770–1788). All were interred in underground crypts constructed beneath the northern and southern naves of the cathedral that appeared in the 17th-18th centuries. This burial method was adopted from the practices of Roman Catholic churches in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth.

After the monastery was abolished in 1786, its buildings were taken over by the imperial administrative institutions (in particular, the building of the Chernihiv Collegium became the seat of the provincial treasury). The Borys and Hlib Cathedral also underwent significant changes. During projects to improve its heating, the ceiling was lowered, the iconostasis was replaced, and the polygonal apse was dismantled. Overall, the church was remodeled into the Classical style, and its walls were decorated with paintings modeled on those of the Volodymyr Cathedral in Kyiv during the 1880s and 1890s.

After the Bolshevik occupation of Ukraine and the establishment of the Ukrainian SSR, the Borys and Hlib Cathedral in the 1920s–1940s successively belonged to the Ukrainian Autocephalous Orthodox Church (1921–1925) and the Renovationist Synodal community of the Russian Orthodox Church (1925–1930). After the church was closed, it was repurposed as a warehouse for dried vegetables and tobacco products.

During the Second World War, the church was damaged by an aerial bomb on 23–25 August 1941. After the war and the completion of extensive archaeological investigations by Mykola Kholostenko (in 1947–48 and 1956–58), the Borys and Hlib Cathedral was reconstructed with an emphasis on restoring its original appearance from the Kyivan Rus’ period. As a result, its appearance as a monument of Ukrainian Baroque architecture, as well as the structures from the time of Hetman Ivan Mazepa, were completely lost. The only reminder of the Ukrainian Cossack era is the magnificent Royal Doors of the iconostasis, which are now a highlight of the museum exhibition in the cathedral.

This monument serves as an essential material source for studying the regional characteristics of 12th-century Rus' architecture. It is equally important for understanding how early Rus' ecclesiastical buildings were transformed within the Ukrainian cultural tradition of the early modern era.

**Further Reading**

Kholostenko, Mykola. «Исследования Борисоглебского собора в Чернигове» [_Studies of the Borys and Hlib Cathedral in Chernihiv_]. Советская археология [_Soviet Archeology_] 2 (1967): 188-210.

The paper presents the results of archaeological research of the cathedral in the 1950s, supporting a hypothesis about its initial forms and the general idea of restoring its original look. The author of the paper is the principal researcher and the author of the reconstruction project.

Travkina, Olha. _Борисоглібський собор міста Чернігова: 900 років історії_ [_The Borys and Hlib Cathedral of Chernihiv: 900 Years of History_]. Chernihiv: Scriptorium, 2025.

This scholarly monograph offers a comprehensive study of the history and architectural transformations of the Borys and Hlib Cathedral throughout the 900 years of its existence. The landmark is presented through its various roles: from a princely house church, a monastic shrine, a cathedral, and a food warehouse, to its current status as a church-museum.
