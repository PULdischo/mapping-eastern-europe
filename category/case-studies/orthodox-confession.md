_By Maksym Yaremenko | National University of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy_

**Description**

The _Orthodox Confession of Faith_ (or _Orthodox Confession_) is a fundamental theological synthesis by 17th-century Orthodox intellectuals of Kyiv. It was compiled under the direction of Kyiv Metropolitan Peter Mohyla (1596–1647). This hierarch came from a Moldavian aristocratic family (his father was for some time a ruler of Wallachia and Moldavia). Peter belonged to a new generation of well-educated and dynamic hierarchs. Thanks to his reforms, the Kyiv Metropolitanate became a powerful Orthodox Church within the Orthodox ecumene. Historians have referred to the period of his activity and the subsequent decades in the history of the Ukrainian Church and culture as the “Mohyla” and “post-Mohyla” eras. 

_Orthodox Confession_ was originally written in Latin and consists of three parts, resembling the structure of Catholic catechisms with the division corresponding to the three theological virtues: faith, hope, and love. The text is structured as questions and answers: 126 questions and answers in the first part, 63 in the second, and 72 in the third. The first, longest part, establishes the grounds of the faith based on the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed. Some prayers and the doctrine of the Holy Sacraments are explained in relation to the articles of the Credo, and the Church’s commandments are annotated regarding the necessity of prayer, regular penance and communion, the observance of feasts and fasts, proper marriage, avoiding contact with heretics, obedience to spiritual authority, etc. The second part discusses the Lord’s prayer and explains nine of the Beatitudes. The third part explains the Decalogue and provides a classification of virtues and sins.

To prove their arguments, the authors draw upon Biblical texts, the works of the Church Fathers and contemporary ecclesiastical writers (ex: Patriarch Jeremiah II Tranos of Constantinople, d. 1595), the Apostolic Canons, and the decrees of Ecumenical and local councils. The compilers also drew upon the experience of post-Tridentine reformed Catholicism. As researchers suggest, this provided the Metropolitan of Kyiv with greater opportunities to systematize doctrine and utilize more refined conceptual definitions. At the same time, it facilitated the adoption of some elements of Catholic theology. Among other things, the _Orthodox Confession_ accepted the Catholic doctrine of _intentio_ (the obligation of intent of clergy during the celebration of the Sacraments) or the possibility, in exceptional cases, of laypeople administering the Sacrament of baptism. 

Moreover, Mohyla rejected the doctrine of the _filioque_, affirmed the communion of the Body and Blood of Christ by the laity, and did not agree with the primacy of the Pope. The _Orthodox Confession_ also has an anti-Protestant orientation. In particular, it clearly emphasizes the necessity of good works for salvation; it underscores that the Virgin Mary is the Theotokos and generally addresses the question of the physicality of Jesus Christ; it explains the necessity of venerating saints and icons, how the interpretation of the Bible was to be solely the prerogative of the Church, and so on.

The National Library of France holds a manuscript copy containing the Greek and Latin texts of the _Orthodox Confession_, which was sent from Istanbul to Paris in 1673 by the French ambassador Charles Noitel at the request of French Catholics. At that time, disputes were ongoing in Europe between Catholics and Protestants regarding the Eucharist, and there was interest in the Orthodox position on this issue. It is suggested that the Latin-language text sent from Constantinople is the closest to the original version of Peter Mohyla’s _Orthodox Confession_. The Kyiv and Iași original manuscript versions of the work are unknown to researchers; they may be lost, or still await discovery.

**Significance**

The _Orthodox Confession_ is the first theological work of the Kyiv Orthodox Metropolitanate to receive conciliar approval and to become the official catechism of the Orthodox Church. Its publication was one of the key steps in the confessional reforms of Kyiv Metropolitan Peter Mohyla, particularly his sustained efforts to systematize Orthodox doctrine. Previous attempts to establish the doctrine of the Orthodox Church, undertaken in the Kyiv Orthodox Metropolitanate during the late 16th century, were not as comprehensive (they consisted of short texts) or contained contradictory theological statements. Scholars suggest that the Kyiv hegumen and rector of the Kyivan Collegium, Doctor of Theology Isaiah Trofymovych (d. 1651), was involved in the compilation of the _Orthodox Confession_. First and foremost, this shows that Mohyla did not compile the _Orthodox Confession_ on his own. Furthermore, Isaiah is perhaps the only Kyiv intellectual of that time who was specifically referred to as a “Doctor of Theology,” which means that he must have gone through the formal procedures for obtaining the degree. In turn, both the text itself and the individuals involved in its compilation provide further insight into the Kyiv theological milieu of that time.

The _Orthodox Confession_ was discussed at the council in Kyiv in September 1640. It was then reviewed at the council convened by Patriarch Parthenios I of Constantinople in Iași (at that time the capital of the Principality of Moldavia, now in Romania) in October 1642. The text was translated into Greek and edited by the Greek theologian and patriarchal exarch Meletius Syrigos (1585–1663), who, in particular, removed the sections resembling the Catholic doctrine of Purgatory and clarified the formulation regarding the time of transubstantiation (the Eucharistical transformation of bread and wine into the body and blood of Jesus Christ ). The revised Greek version of the _Orthodox Confession_, prepared in Iași, was approved by Patriarch Parthenios of Constantinople in March 1643, and was also accepted by other Eastern patriarchs.

On the initiative of the first dragoman (diplomat and advisor, fluent in several languages) at the Ottoman Court, Panaiotis Nicusios, and through the mediation of the Dutch envoy to Constantinople, Joris Croock, the _Orthodox Confession_ was first published in 1666 in Greek _in octavo_ by the publisher Joan Blaeu in Amsterdam (extremely rare, Utrecht University Library, labeled 'E. oct. 956 rariora’). In 1695, a Latin version was published in Leipzig, and in 1696, a Church Slavonic version appeared in Moscow where the Eucharistic discussion was taking place. Subsequently, the book was reprinted numerous times by various publishers, including in Kyiv.

Not willing to wait for the publication of the complete _Orthodox Confession_, the printing of which had been delayed due to a change of the Patriarch of Constantinople, Mohyla published a short version of the work in Kyiv in 1645,  in Ukrainian (“the Ruthenian dialect”) and Polish. Both versions are _in octavo_, titled _A Collection of Brief Teachings on the Articles of the Orthodox-Catholic Christian Faith_. In 1646, the short version was also published in Cyrillic in Ukrainian in Lviv _in quarto_. The editions of 1645 have tenuous differences among them, and indicate the preservation of some theological interpretations that had been replaced in Iași. In particular, Mohyla did not change his view, which was close to the Catholic interpretation, regarding the moment of transubstantiation. It was intended that the shorter and simplified catechism would be used in schools, for all interested Orthodox Christians, and also as a polemical tool (primarily the Polish-language text) to demonstrate the true Orthodox faith to confessional opponents.

A short version was published in Church Slavonic in Moscow in 1649. The book also included other catechetical texts. As in the later Church Slavonic edition of the full version of the _Orthodox Confession_ in 1696, the Moscow editors made some changes in accordance with Moscow religious traditions. For example, they retained baptism by immersion in water only while Kyiv intellectuals also wrote about the possibility of baptism by pouring water. In total, during the 17th and 18th centuries, there were 25 editions of the short catechism, including in Romanian and Greek. The text was used by Russian Old Believers, and in 1788, it was even published by a Uniate printing house in Supraśl (now in Poland).

Over the next two centuries, the _Orthodox Confession_ served as the basis for other theological dogmatic works. The relations between various manuscript copies, the circulation of printed editions in different languages, and the presence of Catholic theological concepts in 17th and 18th-century Kyiv theology require more detailed study.
