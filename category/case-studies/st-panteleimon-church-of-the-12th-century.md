*By Myroslav Voloshchuk | Vasyl Stefanyk Carpathian National University (Ivano-Frankivsk, Ukraine)*

**Description**

The only church to have survived from the 12th century in the Halych region is dedicated to St. Panteleimon and is located in the village of Shevchenkove (Panteleimon hillfort), three kilometers from Halych in a north-west direction. Scholars have hypothesised that the church might have been built during the reign of  Prince Yaroslav Volodymyrkovych (r. cca 1152/3–1187), or during the reign of his son Volodymyr (r. 1189–1198/9) of the Rostislavovid dynasty in the second half of the 12th century or in the time of the Volhynian Prince Roman Mstyslavovych, who ruled in Halych during 1188 and 1198/9–1205. St. Panteleimon is a Romanesque church. Several ruling dynasties of Halych used it as a family church at least during the first half of the 13th century. The size of the temple is 17,18×17,10 m (without apses). Firstly, this church belonged to the Halych bishopric of the Kyivan Metropolis of the Patriarchate of Constantinople. The dedication of the church was changed from St. Panteleimon to St. Stanislav in the 14th century, and the temple became a summer residence for the Latin bishops of Lviv. During a short time in the 15th century the church was used by the Armenian community of Halych. Later, according to the rescript of King Sigmund III (1587-1632) since May 5, 1595, the convent of St. Stanislav church was passed to the monastery of the Franciscan order, which took care of the church until the end of World War II. During the Soviet times the church was closed and used as storage. In the 1990s, the church was returned to its original dedication of St. Panteleimon and after the restoration in 1998 it was reblessed as a Ukrainian Greek-Catholic temple. Today it is a four-column, three-apses church with a central dome. The western portal is its most valuable and richest part by form and decoration. 

St. Panteleimon church is an architectural monument of national importance and a part of the National Reserve “*Ancient Halych*”.

**Significance** 

St. Panteleimon church was studied by different scholars from Ukraine, Poland, Russia, Armenia, etc. Enormous progress in its study was made by Dr.Joseph Pelenskyy. Inspecting the church in 1909 he found numerous inscriptions from the 12th–19th centuries on the walls inside and outside of the church. These inscriptions were made in multiple languages (Old Slavic, Latin, Armenian, Polish, later – Ukrainian) over a period of time. The earliest dates back to 1194. The comprehensive analysis of graffiti from the walls of St. Panteleimon church was made by the Ukrainian scholar from Kyiv Vyacheslav Korniienko in 2018. The results of his study were published in a special volume of the collection of scientific works *“Halych”*. 

Between 1200 and 1213 (with interruptions), one of the fragments of the Life-Giving Cross of the Lord was enshrined in the church as a family relic of the Romanovich dynasty. It was a relic belonging to the second wife of Prince Roman Mstyslavovych, whose name we do not know. The church was his family church and his palace was built on the Panteleimon hillfort. A fragment of the Cross arrived in Halych as part of the princess’s wedding dowry ca. 1200. But after the family moved in 1213 to Volodymyr in Volhynia, this relic never returned to Halych. The True Cross became a family relic and was in the family treasury until April 1340, when the King of Poland Casimir III robbed it from Lviv - new capital of the Romanovich dynasty (from the second half of the 13th century onward).

St. Panteleimon temple is a monumental cross-dome church with the refined white-stoned screw-thread and numerous drawings and graffitis, which are the glorious and tragic witnesses of over 800 years of history. 

When Halych and the Halych land became a significant part of the Kingdoms of Hungary and later Poland in the second half of the 14th century, St. Panteleimon temple changed its dedication to the Latin St. Stanislav catholic archbishop’s cathedral, probably ca. 1367. Later the Latin parish was transferred to Halych in 1427. The Lustration of Halych *starosta* noted the church in 1532, 1552 and 1557.  It specifically stated that the temple “stands alone on the fields  built with square stone.” The reason for such desolation was that in the first half of the 15th century the center of the Latin catholic archbishopric was transferred from Halych to Lviv. However, twice a year on May 8 (the feast day of St. Stanislav) and on August 9 (the feast day of St. Panteleimon), the liturgy was celebrated here.

Being in the hands of the Franciscans since 1595, the former St. Panteleimon church endured different destructions. During the march of the Turks and Tatars on Halych in 1676 it was partly pronged by Ibrahim-Pasha Shaytan. A large fire in 1802 inflicted great harm to the church, destroying  a lot of valuable documents. However, World War I inflicted the most destruction to the site. During a gun fire in 1915 the western portal was almost fully destroyed, except for two caps in the left columns. A western facade, roof, interior, and the top of the bell tower were damaged by the explosion. After the restoration, which was finished in 1926, the church was almost returned to its previous outlook, except for the dedication, leaving the main portal still unrestored. During the restoration in the interior of the temple, a plaster was taken from the walls and the ashlar stone was opened. Under the stratum the tracks of the mural painting (possibly, from the 17th century) and on the stone blocks the graffiti were discovered. 

The columns of the western portal of the church are unique and authentic. They were not blasted by artillery bombardment in 1915. The columns support a frieze with the fretted decorative pattern as pelmets. The portal completes the archivolt with four ledges that fit the columns. The southern portal is more restrained in relation to sculptural decoration, but its architectural structure is derived from Western models. The form of ledges fits into the form of the archivolt. This portal was strongly damaged in 1915. Concerning the apses, they are decorated with half circles, on which the arcs belt leans. The fretted caps of the central apse have various carvings. These caps and arcs frieze are authentic. After the restoration, the temple began to look like this: the shaft of the columns consists of three bunches and knot in the middle; on the edges of the plinths under the bases, the so-called “frogs” are cut out.

The last archaeological research of the temple and its surroundings continued once in 1991 and repeatedly after 2005. The floor in the temple was replaced and the crypts were explored during 2020–2021.
