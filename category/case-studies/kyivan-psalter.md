*By Yaroslav Zatyliuk | National University of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy*

**Description**

The Kyivan Psalter is the largest illuminated Psalter manuscript created in Ukraine and across all of Eastern Europe. It contains a large number of miniatures, which stand out among other 14th-century manuscripts for their style and craftsmanship. It reflects the specifically Byzantine tradition of adorning the Gospels and other liturgical books with miniatures. The text is a Church Slavonic translation of the Psalter from Greek (a total of 151 psalms). Since 1932, the book has been part of the collection of the National Library of Russia in Saint Petersburg. It was restored in 1963, and in 1978 a high-quality facsimile was published.

Fewer than a dozen manuscripts of illuminated Psalters have survived. Among them, the Kyivan Psalter stands out for its size, which currently measures 30 × 24.5 cm. The original dimensions of the manuscript were larger; its present size is the result of trimming along three edges. The binding is likewise not original; it was produced in the 19th century. Today, the book consists of 229 folios. They are made of high-quality parchment of medium thickness, white, smooth, and well-prepared. The manuscript is written in a formal *ustav* script in brown ink of varying shades. It is decorated with a headpiece, initials, and 306 exquisite miniatures of varying sizes. According to some scholars, there may originally have been 309. Due to these features, the book belongs to the category of highly luxurious manuscripts. As a rule, such manuscripts were produced for church hierarchs (a monastery superior, bishop, or metropolitan) or for a member of the secular elite.

In a note written in cinnabar (kinovari, from Greek – κιννάβαρι, kinnábari) after the main text of the Psalter (fol. 227), it is stated that “the book of King David was copied by the protodeacon Spyridon at the command of the humble lord Michael in the year 1397 in the city of Kyiv.” Traditionally, Spyridon is considered a protodeacon of the Dormition Cathedral in Moscow, while the aforementioned hierarch Michael is identified as the Bishop of Smolensk. The identification of the manuscript in scholarly literature is based on this mention of the city (earlier it was referred to as the “Psalter of Spyridon”). Another extensive later inscription in the book is dated to April 10, 1518. It is placed at the bottom of the first 13 folios. It narrates that Abraham (in baptism Ivan) Yezofovych, the land treasurer of the ruler of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, donated this book to the Church of St. Nicholas in the city of Vilnius “for the salvation of his soul and for eternal remembrance.” This inscription is followed by a customary curse against anyone who would remove the book from the possession of this church. The verso of the manuscript’s title page is decorated with a family coat of arms bearing the initials of Abraham Yezofovych, the aforementioned prominent financial administrator, Grand Treasurer of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania in 1510–1519.

Other inscriptions written in Polish, Latin, and Russian are shorter and date to the 17th to the 19th centuries. These inscriptions reflect changes of ownership and the book’s readership. In particular, an entry from 1881 states that the Russian count Sergey Sheremetev purchased the book from Prince Pavel Vyazemsky and, on February 21 of that year, donated it to the Early Literacy Society.

**Significance**

The Psalter is a canonical book of the Old Testament. In the Middle Ages, it was an important religious text, second only to the Four Gospels. It consists of 151 psalms traditionally attributed to the Israelite king David and other Old Testament prophets. These psalms are primarily devoted to reflections on God’s authority over earthly peoples, the deeds of King David, and his continual *obrasheniya* (appeal) to God, expressing repentance for sins, the search for peace, and calls to righteous living. The book was used for teaching literacy, and it was read during church services in the morning and evening. In addition, the Psalter was traditionally used for divination like fortune-telling.

Given its didactic character, the Psalter was often furnished with illustrations. In the Byzantine tradition, the practice of illustrating liturgical books developed from the 6th century and was firmly established after the end of Iconoclasm in the mid-9th century.

Compared to other illuminated Psalters, the Kyivan Psalter stands out for its unusually large size, the great number of illustrations, as well as the way they are harmoniously integrated with the text and clarify its meaning. References to the appropriate miniatures are indicated within the text, and most of them are also provided with captions.

The significance of the book is determined by the diversity of its themes and the refined iconography of its miniatures. A substantial number of them illustrate various episodes from the life of King David: his anointment as king, military campaigns, encounters with different prophets, prayers, and the glorification of God, among other scenes. Particularly unique are the depictions of King David  writing the book himself, as well as miniatures combining his image with that of Christ. The miniatures also represent all the Old Testament prophets and kings associated with the history of David. In addition, the manuscript richly illustrates themes from the Gospels: scenes of the Virgin Mary such as the Presentation in the Temple and the Visitation, all twelve Great Feasts from the Annunciation to the Ascension of Christ, as well as the twelve apostles and their preaching to the nations. At the same time, a significant number of miniatures are devoted to various saints and their deeds, especially those venerated in the Byzantine tradition. These include depictions of Egyptian and Palestinian monastic ascetics (Anthony the Great, Arsenius the Great, Sabbas the Sanctified, Onuphrius the Great, and Symeon the Stylite), as well as the founders of the Orthodox liturgy (Basil the Great, John Chrysostom, and John the Theologian).

A characteristic feature of the miniatures is their allegorical nature. The artist depicts various animals and plants, which serve as symbolic embodiments of different human qualities. For example, a donkey symbolizes a foolish or irrational person, a bird atop a mountain represents the human soul, and a deer at a watering place signifies a person striving to know God. More complex is the allegory of the history of the Israeli people (fol. 13v, 36 v, 64, 110 v). It is represented by the image of an unfenced tree being attacked by a boar and other beasts, illustrating how the faith of these people was subjected to temptations from outside. Particularly noteworthy are the symbolically charged miniatures dedicated to Zion, depicted as a building or a high mountain accompanied by a medallion with the image of the Mother of God *Nikopoia*; Holy Wisdom (*Sophia*) of God, an angel within the interior of a three-domed church; and the Church of Christ represented by a throne with the apostles beside it and a group of figures symbolizing all the regions of earth.

In terms of iconography and technique, the miniatures of the Kyivan Psalter are comparable to much earlier and well-known illuminated Psalters, such as the so-called London Psalter of 1066 (Lond. Brit. Lib. Add. 19352) and the Barberini Psalter of the third quarter of the 11th century (Vat. Barber. gr. 372) from the Vatican Library. At the same time, stylistically, the miniatures of the Kyivan Psalter differ from all illuminated manuscripts created during the 13th–14th centuries in the territories of the Muscovite and Lithuanian dioceses of the Kyiv Metropolis. However, the manuscript is more similar in the style and iconography of its miniatures to the so-called Greek Baltimore Psalter from the Walters Museum in Baltimore. It was created around 1300, most likely within the territory of modern Greece, where it remained until the late 17th century. Both manuscripts were produced within roughly a century of each other, yet their artists clearly drew on a common high-quality model that has not been preserved from the period of the Komnenian dynasty in Byzantium (1081–1185), an era marked by a renewed cultural flourishing. This comparison suggests that the miniatures of the Kyivan Psalter were executed by a Greek artist trained in Constantinople, based on the shown precision of contours, distinctive color palette, and the use of gold highlights. The master had a single assistant, possibly a pupil and perhaps a local artisan. The assistant was responsible for less than a quarter of the images; his work is less refined and was not further reworked by the principal artist. The production process of the Kyivan Psalter was as follows: first, the scribe copied the Slavonic text, after which the Greek master and his local assistant executed the miniatures.

The Kyivan Psalter is a striking example of cultural transfer from Byzantium and the Balkans to the lands of Eastern Europe in the post-Mongol period. This should be the primary consideration when determining whether the artifact belongs exclusively to the Ukrainian or Russian cultural milieu. Scholarly debates on this matter are based on the stereotype of complete cultural decline in Kyiv after the Mongol conquest, as well as on the interpretation of the colophon cited above regarding the copying of the Psalter in Kyiv in 1397 by the protodeacon Spyridon at the command of Bishop Michael. It has traditionally been considered that Bishop Michael of Smolensk ordered the protodeacon of the Moscow Assumption Cathedral, Spyridon, to copy the Gospel. Both of them were temporarily in Kyiv with Metropolitan Cyprian for one and a half years, between 1396–1397. Russian scholars identify both men as Muscovites and therefore attribute the Kyivan Psalter to Russian cultural artifacts. However, it should be noted that Muscovites alone could not have formed the entourage of Metropolitan Cyprian, who resided in Kyiv with interruptions from 1375 to 1390. Moreover, his protodeacon Arsenius, who originated from Kyiv, was appointed Bishop of Tver in 1390. Attention should also be paid to Greek masters who migrated across various principalities and episcopal seats of the former Rus’ due to the complex situation in the Balkans and Byzantium — especially after the Battle of Kosovo in 1389. At the same time, as the colophon itself indicates, the artifact was created in Kyiv, not Moscow, so the necessary materials (both graphic and textual) were available there, either in the metropolitan scriptorium or in the scriptorium of the Kyiv Caves Monastery.

Unfortunately, no documentary sources indicate the movement of the artifact after its creation in Kyiv. It is generally assumed that it was initially transferred to the cathedral in Smolensk, and from there the book became the property of the local official and later treasurer of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania Abraham Hlembitsky, who then transferred it to a church in Vilnius. There is also the hypothesis that the artifact was created in Kyiv at the request of Archbishop Michael of Bethlehem as a gift for the ruler of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, Vytautas, and that under certain circumstances it came into the possession of Abraham Hlembitsky. Michael, Archbishop of Bethlehem, was a prominent late 14th-century Byzantine diplomat and hierarch under the Patriarchate of Jerusalem. In the 1390s, he was deployed on several crucial diplomatic missions to the Grand Duchy of Lithuania to restore canonical order and navigate the fragmentation of the regional Orthodox metropolises. This recent hypothesis contradicts the traditional view regarding the obstacles to the creation of the Kyivan Psalter, and it should be revisited in future research.

**Further Reading**

Culter, Anthony. “The Marginal Psalter in the Walters Art Gallery: A Reconrideration.” *The Journal of the Walters* *Art Gallery* 35 (1977): 37-61

The study presents the provenance and analyzes the iconography and style of the Baltimore Psalter of 1300, establishing its connection to the Kyiv Psalter. It substantiates the thesis that both manuscripts may share a common 11th-century Greek model. 

Vzdornov, Gerold. *Исследование о Киевской Псалтири* [*A Study of the Kyivan Psalter*]. Moskva: Iskusstvo, 1978.

The study offers the most comprehensive history of the manuscript creation, featuring an exhaustive codicological examination, a detailed description and analysis of its miniatures and their sources, and a definitive assessment of its cultural context.

*Киевская Псалтирь 1397 г. из Государственной Публичной библиотеки имени Михаила Салтыкова-Щедрина в Ленинграде* [*The Kyiv Psalter of 1397 from the Mikhail Saltykov-Shchedrin State Public Library in Leningrad*]. Moskva: Iskusstvo, М., 1978.

Facsimile reproduction of the manuscript.

Zhemaitis, Sergei. “К вопросу о происхождении и бытовании  Киевской Псалтири (1397-1518 гг.).” *Хризограф* [“On the Question of the Origin and Provenance of the Kyiv Psalter.” *Khryzohraf*] 2 (2005): 126-138

A hypothesis has been proposed and substantiates that the creation of the codex is linked to Michael, Archbishop of Bethlehem. The author also argues that the codex, which was commissioned by Archbishop Michael of Bethlehem and completed in Kyiv, was a present for the rulers of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania.

Zholtovskyi, Pavlo. “Київські мініатюри 1397 року.” *Матеріали з етнографії та мистецтвознавства* [“The 1397 Kyiv Psalter miniatures.” *Materialy z etnohrafii ta mystetstvoznavstva*]. 6 (1961): 125-143

The article provides a source-critical analysis of the miniatures of the Kyiv Psalter and defines their place within the Ukrainian cultural heritage. The appendix to the article identifies the themes of all 306 miniatures of the manuscript.

Popova, Olga. “Русская книжная миниатюра 11-15 веков.” *Древнерусское искусство. Рукописная книга* [“Russian Manuscript Illumination of the 11th–15th Centuries”. *Old Rus' Art. The Manuscript Book*] Мoskva: Nauka, 1983, 9-75.

The article provides a comprehensive source-critical analysis of all illuminated manuscripts of Old Rus' from the 11th to the 15th centuries. Among these, the Kyiv Psalter of 1397 is examined, substantiating the hypothesis that a Greek master artist executed the majority of its illustrations. Furthermore, the study analyzes the stylistic features of the miniatures and argues for the existence of an 11th-century Greek model as their primary source.
