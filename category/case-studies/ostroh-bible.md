*By Maksym Yaremenko | National University of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy*

**Description**

The Ostroh Bible (12 August 1581) is one of the most famous examples of Ukrainian early book printing, and one of the most remarkable early Cyrillic publications in the world. It is the first complete edition of the Bible in Church Slavonic, produced by Orthodox printers.

The inscription on the title page reads (as per Mathiesen’s translation): “The Bible, that is the Books of the Old and New Testaments, in the Slavonic tongue, in the redaction translated from the Hebrew tongue into the Hellenic by the Seventy Two theosophical Translators three hundred fifty years before the Incarnation of our Lord God and Savior Jesus Christ, at the desirous command of Ptolemy Philadelphus, King of Egypt, with all possible zeal and diligence was examined and corrected, with the help of God \[in the year] 1581 after the Incarnation of our Lord God and Savior Jesus Christ.”

The Bible was printed by Ivan Fedorovych (Fedorov, d. December 5, 1583 in Lviv), and was produced under the patronage of Prince Vasyl-Kostiantyn of Ostroh (d. 1608). He was a powerful magnate and senator in the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, which at that time included the Ukrainian lands, who was actively involved in church affairs. The book is printed *in folio* in two inks. It consists of 628 folios (1,256 pages) on paper mainly produced in Ukraine and Poland. The last folio is known in two versions: the first one indicates 12 July 1580 as the printing date of the Bible. At that time, however, the edition was not completed. The second indicates 12 August 1581, the correct date. As researchers put it, the edition planned for 1580 was not completed in time, but the last folios that had already been printed were later added to the final edition of 1581. Instead of continuous numbering, the edition uses several paginations: the first eight folios are unnumbered, then follows separate pagination of sheets 1–276, 1–180, 1–30, 1–56, and 1–78. The book consists of 104 quires. The text is arranged in two columns of 50 lines each per page. Four Cyrillic and two Greek fonts are used. The book is decorated with over 1,550 graphic elements of various sizes: *forta*, initials, decorated ends of individual parts, the coat of arms of Prince Vasyl-Kostiantyn of Ostroh, and printer Fedorovych’s mark.

There are verses on the coat of arms of Prince Vasyl-Kostiantyn of Ostroh (“See these signs” / “Зри сіѧ знамєніѧ…:) at the beginning of the book. Next, two prefaces follow. The first one is on behalf of Vasyl’-Kostyantyn of Ostroh and it is partially duplicated in Greek. This text contains valuable references to the history of the book’s preparation. The second preface is addressed to Orthodox readers and finished with verses. Both the second preface and verses were written by Gerasym Danylovych Smotrytskyi (d. 1594 or 1597), one of the intellectuals who prepared and presumably printed the Ostroh Bible; he was also a polemicist writer and rector of the Ostroh school founded around 1576. The second preface discusses the importance of the Holy Scripture. After that, the book contains a list of the books of the Old and New Testaments, then their texts, and at the end of the publication are found a *sobornik,* a calendar of non-movable church feasts, an index of Gospel readings, and Ivan Fedorovych’s colophon which is also duplicated in Greek in the 1581 version.

**Significance**

The Ostroh Bible is the last work of the outstanding East European printer Ivan Fedorovych. The book was published in the press of a well-known Ukrainian cultural center – Ostroh in Volhynia, the main city of the vast estates of the powerful magnate princely family. The publication of the Bible became the main task of scholars who gathered together in Ostroh under the patronage of Prince Vasyl’-Kostyantyn of Ostroh. This group included local Church intellectuals, with among them a Protestant publicist, and Greek theologians. The book was intended to demonstrate the power of the Orthodox Church and the relevance of the Church Slavonic language alongside other old languages of the Holy Scripture in the context of religious polemics of the time, primarily with Catholics. The edition played an important role in the development of the religious culture of the Kyiv Metropolitanate.

The canon of biblical books of the Ostroh edition is somewhat broader than the one used in the Orthodox Church at the time and also differs from the Vulgate. At that time, there was no established canon of biblical books in the Slavic Orthodox world. Even the Bible of Gennadii, the copy list of which was used by the Ostroh intellectuals, contained some Old Testament books borrowed from the Vulgate. The publishers of the Ostroh Bible drew on various sources and created the canon themselves, so to speak, that is, they compiled the list of books on their own. The Old Testament books of the Ostroh edition are based on the Septuagint. The first printed Venetian edition of the Septuagint of 1518 was used in preparing the Ostroh edition, as well as a copy of the the Bible of Archbishop Gennadii (manuscript of 1499), other Church Slavonic manuscripts, and prints of different Biblical texts in Latin, Polish, Czech, and possibly German. The wide range of sources and the high level of the textual criticism make it possible to equate the Ostroh Bible’s Church Slavonic translation with the best Latin, Greek, and Hebrew editions of the Holy Scripture of the 16th century.

The print run of the Ostroh Bible is unknown; it ranges from no more than 1,200 to as many as 4,000 copies according to various estimates. These estimates are based on the fact that, for example, an unusually large number of copies have survived to this day compared to other early printed editions of that time. From later information of the 18th century, when book production methods had not changed yet, we know that a typical print run for books i*n folio* was 1,200 copies. Regarding the Ostroh Bible, it is assumed that additional copies of the book may have been printed. All of this leads to an estimate of the possible number of published copies. It was likely the largest Ukrainian edition of its time. Currently, there are approximately 350 surviving copies in several countries in Europe and North America, especially the United Kingdom, Italy, France, Poland, Russia, the United States, and others. One of the world’s largest collections of copies of the Ostroh Bible is held by the V. I. Vernadsky National Library of Ukraine, with 18 copies.

The Ostroh Bible is the largest of any Cyrillic publications before 1610. It formed the basis for Church Slavonic editions of the Holy Scripture in the 17th, 18th, and 19th centuries in Ukraine and beyond. Copies of the Ostroh Bible circulated far beyond the Kyiv Orthodox Metropolitanate and even beyond the Orthodox ecumene.
