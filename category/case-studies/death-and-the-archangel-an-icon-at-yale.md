<strong>Description</strong><br />
&ldquo;Tremble, my soul!&rdquo; (&Phi;&rho;ί&xi;&omicron;&nu;, &psi;&upsilon;&chi;ή &mu;&omicron;&upsilon;), reads a short text at the bottom of this icon. Above it, a prostrate man repeats the words of the Good Thief (Luke 23:42) &ldquo;Lord, remember me!&rdquo; (&Mu;&nu;ή&sigma;&theta;&eta;&tau;ί &mu;&omicron;&upsilon;, &Kappa;ύ&rho;&iota;&epsilon;). This prayer is uttered with the man&rsquo;s last breath, even as his soul departs in the shape of a small child. His body is depicted frontally, so that we observe it from above, the way the soul itself would see it at the last hour. An armed archangel whom the letter M identifies as Michael plants his feet on the fresh corpse, weighing the man&rsquo;s good works (&tau;ὰ &kappa;&alpha;&lambda;ὰ ἔ&rho;&gamma;&alpha;) against his sins (&tau;ὰ &kappa;&alpha;&kappa;ὰ ἔ&rho;&gamma;&alpha;). A hideous creature to the archangel&rsquo;s left declares that &ldquo;every sinner belongs to me, the devil&rdquo; (Ὁ ἁ&mu;&alpha;&rho;&tau;&omicron;&lambda;ὸ&sigmaf; ἐ&mu;&omicron;ῦ &tau;&omicron;ῦ &delta;&iota;&alpha;&beta;ό&lambda;&omicron;&upsilon; ἐ&sigma;&tau;ί). But this fiend is foiled: the man&rsquo;s good deeds outweigh the bad ones, and a guardian angel (&Phi;[ύ&lambda;&alpha;&xi;]) on the opposite, right-hand side receives the redeemed soul with the words &ldquo;Yet he who repents belongs to God!&rdquo; (Ἀ&lambda;&lambda;ὰ ὁ &mu;&epsilon;&tau;&alpha;&nu;&omicron;ύ&mu;&epsilon;&nu;&omicron;&sigmaf; &epsilon;ἶ&nu;&alpha;&iota; &tau;&omicron;ῦ &Theta;&epsilon;&omicron;ῦ). In heaven, Jesus opens his arms: &ldquo;Today you will be with me in paradise&rdquo; (&Sigma;ή&mu;&epsilon;&rho;&omicron;&nu; &mu;&epsilon;&tau;᾽ ἐ&mu;&omicron;ῦ ἔ&sigma;&epsilon;&iota; ἐ&nu; &tau;ῷ &pi;&alpha;&rho;&alpha;&delta;&epsilon;ί&sigma;ῳ). His meaning (Luke 23:43) is not obscured by the painter&rsquo;s imperfect spelling (&tau;ὸ &pi;&alpha;&rho;&alpha;&delta;ί&sigma;&omicron;).<br />
The style suggests work of the early 18th century, probably by a Cretan artist. Judging from its small size (29.2 &times; 22.2 cm), the icon was used for private prayer at home, as a personal memento mori. Its back is almost empty, except for a roughly painted cross and for the short inscription IC XC NIKA (&ldquo;Jesus Christ is victorious&rdquo;); there are also traces from two transversely nailed slats, now removed, which once restrained the wood from warping. The edges are covered with red as a kind of frame that protects the painter&rsquo;s work. His gold and tempera pigments are on the whole well preserved. The devil, however, has been defaced in an act of pious vandalism.</p>

<p><strong>Significance</strong><br />
This is an icon with a message: it urges us to live virtuously by reminding us that &ldquo;man is destined to die once and after that to face judgment&rdquo; (Hebrews 9:27), that &ldquo;the wages of sin is death&rdquo; (Romans 6:23), that &ldquo;the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord&rdquo; (ibid.). To be effective, the reminder must be vivid. Vividness is achieved through the use of gold and of contrasting colors and by overloading the composition&rsquo;s right, symbolically good side. (Note that orientation is determined from within, so that &ldquo;right&rdquo; corresponds to Christ&rsquo;s right hand rather than ours.) The two nearly identical angels&rsquo; heads create emphasis by repetition; Michael&rsquo;s raised sword, the forearms of the guardian angel, and the upright anthropomorphic soul form parallel lines; the rich drapery of the angels&rsquo; clothing contrasts with the flaccid flesh of the naked devil. Besides being emphatic, the icon&rsquo;s message is focused (&ldquo;Tremble, <em>my</em> soul!&rdquo;, &ldquo;Lord, remember <em>me</em>!&rdquo;, &ldquo;Today <em>you</em> will be with me in paradise&rdquo;): I am witnessing not another man&rsquo;s death but my own. The architectural background itself draws me &ldquo;into&rdquo; the image by means of several doorways and receding perspective.<br />
The painter&rsquo;s use of pictorial space and of visual parallel/contrast as rhetorical devices, as well as his rather naturalistic shading (most prominent in the clouds, in the drapery, and in Michael&rsquo;s breastplate), recall West-European art. The Baroque style reached many Greek regions through Venice, which ruled Crete until 1669 and held the Ioanian Islands up to 1797 (see <strong><a href="https://mappingeasterneurope.princeton.edu/item/the-icon-of-our-lady-skopiotissa-savina-monastery.html">Our Lady Skopiotissa</a></strong>). But Italian painting contains no direct parallels for the scene depicted in our icon. Its central motif seems to have been invented by a Greek artist named Antonios Mitaras, whose <strong><a href="http://eib.xanthi.ilsp.gr/gr/showpic.asp?picpath=64_8bit_PE-IC6-DS2-Q3&amp;curtable=icons&amp;currecord=64&amp;vorder=1&amp;vmode=first&amp;fbclid=IwAR1F9_AG-xMySEzhNXOMUCJMdmj_WyKkKAk2SQmMpMsmdWDhMZalWmE_Al4">sole surviving work</a></strong> also shows Archangel Michael receiving the soul of a dying man and also carries the exhortation &ldquo;Tremble, my soul, at the things you behold!&rdquo; (&Phi;&rho;ί&xi;&omicron;&nu;, &psi;&upsilon;&chi;ή &mu;&omicron;&upsilon;, &tau;ὰ ὁ&rho;ώ&mu;&epsilon;&nu;&alpha;). Nothing is known about Mitaras&rsquo;s biography, but his manner clearly predates that of the anonymous Yale painter.</p>
