*By Yaroslav Zatyliuk | NationalUniversity of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy*

**Description**

The *Peresopnytsia Gospel* is the oldest known translation of the Holy Scriptures into the Old Ukrainian literary language. The translation was produced between 1556 and 1561, which is also when the manuscript was created. The book is a valuable source for studying the history of language and culture, particularly the spread of Renaissance cultural practices and the ideas of the European Reformation in 16th-century Ukraine. Today, the *Peresopnytsia Gospel* is a national treasure, as all newly elected Presidents of Ukraine take their oath of office upon it along with the text of the Constitution of Ukraine during the inauguration ceremony in the Ukrainian Parliament.

The *Peresopnytsia Gospel* is a large manuscript book measuring 380 × 240 mm, in folio format, weighing more than 9 kg, and comprising 482 folios. It is bound in oak boards covered with the original green velvet, now worn with age. The parchment is high-quality, but its thickness varies.

The entire manuscript is written in a combination of formal uncial and semi-uncial scripts, with occasional elements of cursive writing. One distinctive feature of the handwriting is that some letter forms imitate old and medieval models, while others resemble the typefaces of 16th-century Cyrillic printed books. The text is written in dark brown and brown ink. The manuscript also makes extensive use of cinnabar (a red pigment), employed for headings, chapter initials, marginal notes, punctuation marks, and references in the indexes.

In terms of content, the book is a typical Orthodox Tetraevangelion, consisting of the texts of the four Evangelists—Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John. The volume opens with the preface from the *Blagovisnyk* (Commentary on the Gospels) of Theophylact of Bulgaria, Archbishop of Ohrid at the turn of the 12th century. The scribe supplied each Gospel with an index of its principal sections and a colophon. At the end of the manuscript, there is an afterword and a menologion (a calendar of saints arranged chronologically throughout the year, beginning in September).

Among traditional Orthodox Gospel Books, the *Peresopnytsia Gospel* stands out because its text is divided into chapters, each accompanied by a brief summary of its content, an annotation known as a *summarium*, written in smaller script. This feature links the manuscript to contemporary 16th-century translations of the Holy Scriptures produced in Bohemia and Poland.

The text is embellished with exquisite miniatures of the four Evangelists preceding each Gospel text, as well as masterfully executed headpieces, initials, and ornamental decorations.

The manuscript also contains scribal records that allow scholars to reconstruct its history. These notes indicate that work on the book began on 15 August 1556 in the Monastery of the Holy Trinity in the village of Dvorets, in the Khmelnytsk region today. During that year, the first 155 folios were completed, containing the Gospel of Matthew and half of the Gospel of Mark. After a pause of several years, work resumed at a different location—the Monastery of the Nativity of the Virgin in Peresopnytsia now in the Rivne region. This later stage gave the manuscript its modern name. The book was completed there on 29 August 1561.

According to the colophons, the work was directed by Archimandrite Hryhorii of the Peresopnytsia Monastery and the scribe Mykhailo Vasylievych, son of the Archpriest of Sianok. Evidently, Hryhorii was the translator and supervisor of the copying process. It remains unknown who assisted them or who produced the miniatures and decorative elements. One of the miniatures bears the date 1556.

The records in the manuscript (folio 481) suggest that the project was financed by Princess Anastasiia Yuriiivna Zaslavska-Holszanska, widow of Prince Kuzma Ivanovych Zaslavsky (r. 1511-1556), who had by then become abbess of the Dvorets Monastery under the name Paraskeva. Later, the work was supported by the Czartoryski princes, on whose lands the Peresopnytsia Monastery was located.

Nothing is known of the manuscript’s whereabouts after the Czartoryski family converted to Catholicism and transferred the monastery to the authority of the Jesuit College of Klevan in 1630. The next reliable information comes from an inscription at the beginning of the manuscript, stating that in 1701 Hetman Ivan Mazepa (r. 1687–1709), ruler of the Cossacks, donated it to the Cathedral of Pereiaslav on the occasion of the establishment of a bishopric there under Bishop Zakharia Kornylovych (r. 1700–1715).

By the 1790s, the manuscript was no longer used in church services and was transferred to the Pereiaslav Seminary. In 1862, it was moved to Poltava together with the seminary. In the early 1870s, it was sent to St. Petersburg for inspection by the emperor, and in 1873 it became the property of Prince Peter Georgievich of Oldenburg (r. 1812-1881). After his death, his widow returned the manuscript to Poltava in 1887. In 1941, it was placed in the collections of what is now the National Preserve “Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra.” Following the efforts of the distinguished bibliographer Serhii Maslov, it was transferred in 1948 to the Institute of Manuscripts of the Vernadsky National Library of Ukraine, where it remains today (Fond I, No. 15512).

**Significance**

The *Peresopnytsia Gospel* is first and foremost a monument of the Ukrainian literary language. It is a translation into Old Ukrainian from the Bulgarian recension of Church Slavonic, the language of the liturgy and written culture of Rus’ from the 11th century onward. In essence, the translation represents a modernization of this language into the literary Old Ukrainian of the 16th century, while incorporating elements of the contemporary spoken language. Such features are especially numerous in the Gospel of Luke. In the other three Gospels, Church Slavonic words are frequently accompanied by glosses in the spoken 16th-century Ukrainian vernacular. The text of the *Peresopnytsia Gospel* thus combines three linguistic registers: Church Slavonic in its Bulgarian recension, literary Old Ukrainian, and spoken Ukrainian. This linguistic blend defines the manuscript’s Ukrainian identity.

The first scholar to emphasize this Ukrainian identity of the *Peresopnytsia Gospel* was Osip Bodiansky (1808–1877), the eminent Slavist and secretary of the Moscow Society of Russian History and Antiquities. In 1838, as a graduate scholar of Moscow University, he described the manuscript as a Church Slavonic translation of the Holy Scriptures distinguished by the inclusion of spoken Ukrainian vocabulary. According to Bodiansky, the Ukrainian linguistic features of the text were “as clear as the stars in the heavens.” A few years later, Taras Shevchenko (1814–1861), Ukraine’s national poet and founder of modern Ukrainian literature, examined the manuscript and likewise recognized its Ukrainian linguistic foundation.

Linguists have identified numerous lexical, phraseological, and syntactic features in the text that contributed to the formation of the Ukrainian literary language. Significantly, the compilers of the *Dictionary of the Ukrainian Historical Language* published in 1930–1932 drew upon approximately 10,000 words from the *Peresopnytsia Gospel*. 

The manuscript is also an important witness to the development of Ukrainian culture and its European background. It demonstrates the adoption in Ukraine of the trend that emerged in late fifteenth-century Europe: translating the Old and New Testaments into vernacular languages, a development that became one of the principal driving forces of the Reformation.

The most obvious model for the creators of the *Peresopnytsia Gospel* was the work of Francysk Skaryna, who printed twenty-two books of the Old Testament in Prague between 1517 and 1519 under the title *The Ruthenian Bible*. Skaryna himself had been influenced by the Czech Bible printed in Venice in 1506. Under this direction, several Ukrainian monastic scriptoria in Volhynia and Podillia produced translations of other church books into the “simple Ruthenian language” (*prosta mova*) during the 1570s–1590s, including the *Song of Songs*, the *Apostolos*, the *Psalter*, and various editions of didactic Gospels.

Like contemporary Czech and Polish printed editions of the Scripture, the creators of the *Peresopnytsia Gospel* modified the traditional structure of Orthodox Gospel Books by dividing the text into chapters and providing each chapter with a brief summary. In the colophon on folios 443–443v, this innovation is described as a practical aid that enables Orthodox clergymen to locate arguments more quickly during religious polemics. This suggests that the manuscript may be viewed as a response by Ukrainian Orthodox intellectuals to the challenges posed by the growing influence of Catholicism and Protestantism in Ukraine and the resulting competition for believers’ allegiance.

The *Peresopnytsia Gospel* is universally regarded as a masterpiece of 16th-century Ukrainian book art. Influenced by printed books featuring Italian Renaissance ornamental motifs, the artist adapted local Ukrainian iconographic traditions in depicting the Evangelists. This is evident in the execution of drapery folds, the proportions of the figures, their facial features, and the choice of color scheme. The miniatures of the Evangelists in the *Peresopnytsia Gospel* are typologically similar to the depictions of saints in famous 16th-century icons from churches in the Lviv region.

The borders surrounding the Evangelist miniatures are filled with elaborate vegetal compositions incorporating roses, acanthus motifs, and putti, all characteristic of Italian Renaissance ornamentation.

The manuscript’s script likewise represents a synthesis of local and European Renaissance traditions. It combines formal uncial and semi-uncial writing, with some letter forms imitating the typefaces of Skaryna’s printed books and others reflecting traditional Ukrainian and Balkan manuscript conventions. Particularly distinctive is the handwriting used for the chapter summaries: a cursive script, known as “small writing” (*drobne pysmo*), characterized by smaller, running letters. Comparison with other manuscripts produced in Volhynia during the second half of the 16th century has led scholars to identify the emergence of a new Ukrainian handwriting style.

The *Peresopnytsia Gospel*, created in the mid-16th century, thus testifies to the dissemination and adaptation of the principal trends of the European Renaissance and Reformation within Ukrainian society. This involved the ruling elite, who commissioned translations of Scripture; learned churchmen, who produced those translations; artists and illuminators, who decorated the manuscript; and scribes, who carried out the laborious process of creating this masterpiece of book culture.

Overall, this manuscript holds great promise for studying the history of transformations in the traditional Church Slavonic language, alongside the development of the Ukrainian vernacular and literary languages. In addition, the object is crucial for investigating the dynamics of cultural transfer, specifically the impact of European artistic and printing trends on the key agents of 16th-century Ukrainian culture.

**Further Reading**

*Пересопницьке Євангеліє 1556-1561: Дослідження. Транслітерований текст. Словопокажчик* [*The Peresopnytsia Gospels 1556–1561: Studies. Transliterated Text. Index of Words*]. Kyiv: Natsionalna akademiia nauk Ukrainy, 2001.

The publication consists of three components. The first part comprises scientific studies of the manuscript, which provide a detailed analysis of the history of its creation, as well as its linguistic, orthographic, textual, and artistic features. Separate studies are dedicated to the specific characteristics of its language, handwriting, and the material analysis of the manuscript itself. The second part of the publication is the edition of the transliterated text, which is an accurate reproduction of the original Gospel text using the characters matching the graphics of that period, while preserving linguistic nuances to the maximum extent. The third part contains a complete word index for the text, indicating the forms and contexts of usage of each word. The edition is illustrated with color reproductions of the original pages and miniatures.

*Borys Gudziak Crisis And Reform. The Kyivan Metropolitanate, The Patriarchate Of Constantinople And The Genesis Of The Union Of Brest*. Cambridge, MA : Ukrainian Research Institute, Harvard University : Distributed by Harvard University Press, 1998.	

The monograph is a comprehensive study of socio-religious changes in Ukraine caused by the spread of the phenomenon of printing and Reformation movements, as well as the general circumstances surrounding the conclusion of the Union of Brest (1596). The author addresses the practice of translating the books of the Holy Scripture and, in particular, the manuscript of the Peresopnytsia Gospels (pp. 105-107). The book provides an understanding of the overall background against which the monument emerged.
