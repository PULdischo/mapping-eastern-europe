*By Martin-Oleksandr Kisly | HistoryDepartment of National University of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy (NaUKMA)*

**Description**

The Genoese Fortress in Sudak (medieval Soldaia or Sougdaia), Crimea, is a monumental defensive complex primarily constructed by the Genoese during their administration of the Crimean coastline, between 1371–1469. Sudak is the name of the city where the fortress is situated; Soldaia is connected only to its Genoese history. Occupying a 30-hectare site atop a steep conical hill, the fortress utilizes the natural precipice of the Kyz-Kule-Burun (Fortress Mountain) for its defense. The fortress is organized into two distinct levels. The lower defensive belt, extending approximately to one kilometer, consists of a massive outer wall 6to 8meters high and 1.5 to 2meters thick. This curtain wall was reinforced by fourteen 15-meter combat towers and a Main Gate complex. The upper tier comprises the Consul's Castle and towers connected by a wall running along the mountain crest, culminating in the Watchtower (Maiden Tower) at the mountain's peak. Additionally, the site contains a rectangular stone cistern with a capacity of 185 m³, designed for water storage during droughts or sieges.

A distinguishing feature of the site is its epigraphic heritage in Latin; many towers are named after the Genoese consuls who commissioned them, often marked by heraldic slabs featuring the Genoese cross. The complex includes the Padishah-Jami mosque (13th century), which was later adapted as a Catholic church and eventually a Russian Orthodox chapel after the 1783 annexation of Crimea by the Russian Empire. 

**Significance**

The Genoese fortress of Sudak is an exceptional site for analyzing the intersection of Mediterranean maritime trade and transcontinental commerce in the 14th and 15th centuries. As a key administrative and economic hub within the Gazaria – the collective name for the Genoese colonies in the Black Sea – Soldaia served as a vital terminus for the Silk Road . Its strategic location facilitated the exchange of goods between the Mongol Empire (specifically the Golden Horde) and the Mediterranean world. While Caffa (Feodosia) served as the administrative capital of the Gazaria, Soldaia was its primary military and commercial gateway, often acting as the first point of contact for ships arriving from Constantinople and the Italian republics.

Methodologically, the fortress offers a critical case study in the history of long-term strategic topography. Long before the Genoese arrival, the site served as a vital lookout for the Bosporan Kingdom and later the Roman Empire (1st-3rd century). The site did not remain dormant in the intervening centuries; its natural harbor and defensive cliffs were continuously utilized by successive regional powers. It was fortified by the Byzantines in the 6th century and subsequently governed by the Khazars, Cumans, and the Golden Horde. By the 12th and 13th centuries, the city – then known as Sugdea – had evolved into a highly developed Silk Road emporium under Venetian commercial dominance. Therefore, when the Genoese captured the city in 1365 and repurposed it as Soldaia, they were not building on a blank slate, but rather seizing a fiercely contested international hub. This continuous chain of occupation establishes Sudak not as a colonial novelty of the late Middle Ages, but as a site of “topographical determinism,” where the natural features of the “Fortress Mountain” necessitated its use by every major power seeking to control the Black Sea.

The architectural layout of the site reflects a specific strategy of territorial control where a Latin administration managed a diverse, multi-ethnic population of Greeks, Armenians, and other local groups through fortified enclaves. The epigraphic evidence found on the fortress towers, specifically the heraldic slabs of the Genoese consuls, provides a detailed chronological record of the Republic of Genoa’s political presence. While the installation of such memorial stones was a standard Genoese colonial practice across the Black Sea—also prominent in Kaffa and Cembalo—the extensive survival of these slabs in Soldaia offers an exceptional material timeline of regional investment. These slabs traditionally pair the cross of St. George with the coats of arms of the ruling doge and the local consul. Surviving examples commemorate the tenures of specific administrators, such as Pasquale Giudice (1392), Corrado Cicala (1404), and Bernardo di Franchi di Pagano (1414). Together, they serve as tangible evidence of the aristocratic competition among noble families for control over the lucrative maritime trade.

Furthermore, the site is a significant religious palimpsest that physically reflects the shifting political and confessional allegiances of Crimea. The Padishah-Jami mosque is perhaps the most representative structure in this regard. The building itself – a domed, cubic hall – remains largely intact today. Originally constructed in the 13th century following a Seljuk-influenced architectural prototype, its Islamic function is materially confirmed by a *mihrab* (prayer niche) oriented toward Mecca embedded in its southern wall. During the Genoese period, the building was converted into a Roman Catholic church dedicated to the Virgin Mary; this Latin phase is physically documented by faint fragments of Catholic frescoes that still survive on the interior masonry. Following the Ottoman conquest in 1475, the structure returned to use as a mosque, and after the 1783 annexation by the Russian Empire, it was repurposed as an Orthodox military chapel. The physical survival of this building, alongside the tangible traces of its successive liturgical adaptations, underscores the role of Sudak as a crossroads of civilizations where religious spaces were continually re-inscribed with new meanings.

Finally, the technical sophistication of the fortress, exemplified by the 185 m³ stone cistern, highlights the advanced hydraulic engineering necessary to sustain a permanent population in an arid coastal environment. Similar water-management systems can be found in other Mediterranean contexts, such as the cisterns of Rhodes or Malta, yet the Sudak cistern is specific to the logistical needs of a settlement that faced sieges from both land and sea. In the broader context of Eastern European history, Sudak stands as a physical bridge between the medieval steppe and the maritime empires of the West, representing the peak of Latin architectural and commercial presence in the region before the consolidation of Ottoman power.

The current state of the Genoese Fortress in Sudak is defined by the consequences of the Russian occupation of Crimea since 2014, as a result of which the monument fell under the control of the occupying administration. Improper management and uncoordinated construction on the territory of the reserve led to the collapse of one of the towers in November 2015. Subsequent “restoration” efforts were carried out with gross violations: the use of concrete, modern cement mortar, and the involvement of unqualified workers have led to the distortion of the site's authenticity.

**Further Reading**

Balard, Michel. *La Romanie génoise (XIIe-début du XVe siècle)*. Rome: École française de Rome, 1978. 

This foundational study provides a comprehensive analysis of the administrative and economic structures of the Gazaria colonies. It is essential for understanding the strategic role of Soldaia within Mediterranean trade networks during the 13th and 14th centuries.

Khvalkov, Evgeny. *The Colonies of Genoa in the Black Sea Region: Evolution and Transformation.* New York: Routledge, 2017. 

This work investigates the socio-economic evolution of the Genoese outposts. It is particularly useful for contextualizing the 14th and 15th centuries Crimean settlements within the broader transformations of the late medieval Mediterranean commercial system.

Quirini-Popławski, Rafał. *The Art of the Genoese Colonies of the Black Sea Basin (1261–1475).* Leiden: Brill, 2023.     

This recent volume is indispensable for studying the visual and material culture of the colonies. It examines the synthesis of Italian Gothic forms with local traditions in the architectural and epigraphic remains of the Black Sea outposts.
