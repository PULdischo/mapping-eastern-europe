*By Maksym Yaremenko | National University of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy*

**Description**

Lutsk Castle is one of the oldest surviving castles in Ukraine. The fortifications in medieval Lutsk originally consisted of two parts: the Upper Castle and the Side (*Okolnyi*)Castle.

The Upper Castle is situated on a hill over the Styr River. At the site where it was built, smaller wooden and earthen fortifications surrounding the town’s citadel probably already existed, possibly dating to the late 10th century based on archaeological excavations and research). The Upper Castle was built by Prince Liubartas (d. 1383), son of Grand Duke Gediminas of Lithuania—hence the title “Liubartas’ Castle”. The castle was constructed in several stages. Its fortifications include three towers. From the mid-14th century to the mid-1380s, the wooden elements were gradually replaced with brick ones. First, the Entrance Tower, the prince’s palace adjacent to the Entrance Gate, and a section of the wall on the west side were built using bricks of various sizes. Then, using higher-quality bricks of a more or less uniform size, sections of the eastern and northern walls with loopholes and merlons were constructed, the Entrance Tower was raised, and the southern Styr Tower was laid but only a tier was built. In the late 14th–early 15th centuries, when Lutsk became an important center in the south of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania (the Lithuanian-Ruthenian state), the brick walls with loopholes and merlons finally replaced the wooden walls, and the Bishop’s Tower was built using the highest-quality materials. These works are associated with Vytautas the Great (d. 1430), Grand Duke of Lithuania.

The castle was involved in the struggle between various claimants to the rule of Lutsk in the 1430s. As scholars suggest, the widespread use of firearms prompted the decision to raise the height of the walls and the Styr Tower (like the other two towers, it was given a tent-shaped roof) make arched embrasures for heavy large-caliber guns above the loopholes made for throwing weapons, and to add special wooden parapets on the walls. The thickness of the walls could reach up to approximately 3 meters.

To the west and south of the Upper Castle stood the Side Castle, which was separated by a moat with a drawbridge leading to the Entrance Tower and was significantly larger in area. By the end of the 15th century, the wooden walls of the Side Castle were replaced with brick walls featuring merlons. This reconstruction continued until the mid-16th century. Wooden structures were also added on the brick walls. By the mid-16th century, the Side Castle remained partly brick and partly wood; specifically, four towers were made of wood, and four were made of brick. The *Okolnyi* Castle was also separated from the city by a moat with a drawbridge.

During the second half of the 16th century, the defensive function of Lutsk Castle declined significantly. It, along with other fortifications in Lutsk, was repaired and reinforced in 1667. In particular, additional embrasures for small firearms were installed. In 1781, both castles were severely damaged by fire. From the late 18th century onward, the fortifications finally completely lost their defensive functions.

**Significance**

The founder of Upper Castle, Liubartas, was the son-in-law of Andriy Yuriyovych, Prince of Galicia and Volhynia, and became ruler of Volhynia in 1340. Over time, the city of Lutsk became the main princely residence and its fortifications were necessary due to the war with neighbors. To enhance the fortress’ defensive capabilities, a dam was built on the Styr River to raise the water level and the lower parts of the castle promontory were filled in. In 1429, the castle hosted the so-called Congress of Rulers or Congress of Lutsk, which was attended by rulers or their representatives from Poland, Hungary, Bohemia, the Holy Roman Empire, Denmark, the Holy See, and several other states, as well as the aristocracy and high clergy. It is estimated that the number of visitors exceeded the population of Lutsk at the time. Among the most important events was the coronation of Duke Vytautas as King.

The Upper Castle served as the seat of the secular and ecclesiastical administrative authority, as well as secular and ecclesiastical courts. In 1578, it even housed a tribunal, which was the highest court of appeal for the nobility of the three Ukrainian voivodeships. The archive containing documents was kept there. By the mid-16th century, the Upper Castle complex housed, in addition to the princely palace with frescoes featured in some of its halls, an Orthodox cathedral dedicated to St. John the Theologian. This became the Uniate church from the 18th century onward. The cathedral served as a mausoleum for princes, the nobility, and the higher clergy. 

There were also the large and small wooden bell towers, 7 houses (including a large bishop’s residence), 2 kitchens, a cellar, an armory, a bathhouse, a basement, a prison, and a cemetery. At that time, presumably as a result of the fire of 1539, the palace was being repaired at the expense of Yuri Falchevskyi, the Roman Catholic Bishop of Lutsk, but the reconstruction of this two-story building with a basement was not completed. The presence of property belonging to the Orthodox Bishop of Lutsk and Ostroh in the Upper Castle and attempts to expand it in the late 16th and early 17th centuries led to a conflict with the castle’s administrator.

During the mid-16th century, the Side Castle housed five Orthodox churches, one Roman Catholic church next to which a bell tower and cemetery were built later, some fifty noble estates, and artisan workshops. There was also an execution site there. In the following decades, the number of Roman Catholic Church institutions in the castle increased; in particular, monks from the Jesuit and nuns from the Brigittine orders arrived. The *Okolnyi* Castle housed the residence of the Roman Catholic bishop and a school located at the Holy Trinity Catholic Cathedral. 

Both castles were to be maintained not only at the expense of the Volhynia’s ruler but also by the surrounding nobility and from city profits. From the mid-15th century, the castles were administered by a *starost* – a representative of the highest state authority. From the second half of the 16th century, the region’s nobility held regular local sejms at various locations within both castles – noblemen discussed not only local but also state matters in these local parliaments. 

During the 18th century, stone bell towers were built near the cathedral churches in the Upper and *Okolnyi* Castles. From the late 18th century, judicial and administrative bodies were located in the castles, and later, municipal services. In the 20th and early 21st centuries, conservation and restoration work was carried out in the castle several times.

Today, the Upper Castle, the remains of the *Okolnyi* Castle wall, and the preserved historical sites on the Side Castle’s former location are part of the “Old Lutsk” Historical and Cultural Preserve and are open to visitors.
